11	Abstract

12 Despite remarkable advances in protein structure prediction, a fundamental question
13 remains unresolved: how do proteins fold from unfolded conformations into their native
14 states? Here, we introduce PathDiffusion, a novel generative framework that simulates
15 protein folding pathways using evolution-guided diffusion models. PathDiffusion first
16 extracts structure-aware evolutionary information from 52 million predicted structures
17 the AlphaFold database. Then an evolution-guided diffusion model with a dual-score
18 fusion strategy is trained to generate high-fidelity folding pathways. Unlike existing
19 deep learning methods, which primarily sample equilibrium ensembles, PathDiffusion
20 explicitly models the temporal evolution of folding. On a benchmark of 52 proteins
21 with experimentally validated folding pathways, PathDiffusion accurately reconstructs
22 the order of folding events. We further demonstrate its versatility across four
23 challenging applications: (1) recapitulating Anton's molecular dynamics trajectory for
24 12 fast-folding proteins, (2) reproducing functionally important local folding-unfolding
25 transitions in 20 proteins, (3) characterizing conformational ensembles of 50
26 intrinsically disordered proteins, and (4) resolving distinct folding mechanisms among




27 3 TIM-barrel proteins. We anticipate that PathDiffusion will be a valuable tool for
28 probing protein folding mechanisms and dynamics at scale.

31	Introduction

32 Though protein structure prediction has achieved significant advance in recent years,
33 the problem of protein folding remains a grand challenge. Unraveling protein folding
34 pathway is crucial for understanding its function. Although protein function is generally
35 considered to be associated with its native state, increasing evidence suggests that even
36 non-native states also play important biological roles1, 2. For example, certain folding
37 intermediates may be precursors to amyloid protein aggregation, which is closely
38 associated with several diseases including Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases3, 4.
39 Therefore, exploring the dynamic process and intermediate states of protein folding is
40 of great importance for understanding disease mechanisms and drug design5, 6.
41

42 Traditionally, protein folding research has relied on experimental methods such as
43 hydrogen-deuterium exchange, circular dichroism, and fluorescence spectroscopy7.
44 Although these techniques provide valuable data on protein dynamics, they often
45 require complex procedures and are limited to a small number of proteins, making it
46 difficult to achieve large-scale investigations in a wider range of protein folding
47 systems8, 9. Molecular dynamics (MD) is an important tool for studying folding
48 dynamics. However, it is limited by computational costs, and its time scale is difficult
49 to cover the entire folding process.
50

51 In recent years, generative artificial intelligence has significantly advanced the
52 development of the field of protein structure prediction10, 11 and protein design12, 13.
53 More recently, deep learning models such as BioEmu, AlphaFlow, and DIG have
54 extended these capabilities to generate heterogeneous conformational ensembles14-17.
55 Despite these advances, most existing methodologies still prioritize the characterization




56 of thermodynamic equilibrium states over the reconstruction of continuous folding
57 trajectory. We previously developed FoldPAthreader, which explores potential
58 intermediates through folding force field-based conformation sampling18. Other
59 emerging methods include generative world models, which learn a strategy from latent
60 spatiotemporal representations to drive folding simulations in latent representations19;
61 and the iterative AlphaFold2 strategy, which utilizes implicit energy landscapes to
62 estimate folding pathways based solely on sequence20. Despite these methodological
63 advances, developing a generalizable data-driven framework for folding dynamics is
64 still impeded by fundamental constraints, most notably the scarcity of high-resolution
65 experimental data and the computational infeasibility of generating comprehensive
66 training sets via MD21, 22.
67

68 In this study, we developed PathDiffusion, a novel framework for protein folding
69 pathway prediction using evolution-guided diffusion models. Our method is built on
70 the fundamental relationship between evolution and protein dynamics18, 23. Evaluation
71 on 52 proteins with experimental evidence of folding pathway suggests the superior
72 performance of PathDiffusion. Further applications on four datasets related to folding
73 pathway, including fast-folding proteins from Anton's molecular dynamic simulations,
74 local folding-unfolding transition proteins, intrinsically disordered proteins, and TIM-
75 barrel proteins, confirm that PathDiffusion is promising to explore the landscape
76 protein dynamics.

79	Results

80 Overview of PathDiffusion

81 The overall workflow of PathDiffusion is illustrated in Figure 1. It comprises two main
82 modules: the first module focuses on preparing position-specific noise schedules (PSNS)
83 from evolutionary information, while the second module iteratively generates the




84 folding pathway using evolution-guided diffusion models. In the first module, we begin
85 with an input protein sequence and construct a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) by
86 searching for homologous sequences in the AFDB5024, 25 using MMseqs226 and
87 JackHMMER27. Following this, we obtain a multiple structure alignment (MSTA) by
88 aligning the structure models found in AFDB50. Subsequently, PSNS are derived based
89 on the structural consensus and variations observed in the MSTA, which are used to
90 guide the diffusion process in the second module (see Methods for more detail).
91

92 The second module employs an evolution-guided two-stage diffusion model to simulate
93 the dynamic process of protein folding, transitioning from unfolded to folded states. In
94 the first stage, a sequence-conditional diffusion model is used to generate an initial
95 folding trajectory. It starts from Gaussian noise and performs T steps of evolution-
96 guided reverse denoising. Conserved residues undergo rapid denoising, forming stable
97 local structures in the early steps. Conversely, flexible residues retain noise longer,
98 leading to gradual formation of the global fold as denoising progresses. The resulting T
99 denoised states form a continuous trajectory: the initial state (step 1) represents
100 complete Gaussian noise, while the final state (step T) corresponds to the fully folded
101 structure. The intermediates (steps 2 to T-1) are partially folded, containing varying
102 degrees of noise and local structures, thereby capturing the continuous trajectory of
103 protein folding. In the second stage, an unconditional diffusion model is employed in
104 conjunction with the sequence-conditional model to refine the trajectory dynamics.
105 Adaptive weighting derived from the trajectory in first stage is applied to balance the
106 contributions of both the sequence-conditional and unconditional models. This two-
107 stage  pipeline  generates  continuous  trajectory  of  protein  folding,  allowing  a
108 comprehensive characterization of intermediate states and elucidation of the folding
109 pathway.

113 Performance of PathDiffusion

114 To evaluate PathDiffusion's capability in modeling protein folding pathways, we
115 compiled a benchmark set consisting of 52 proteins with experimentally characterized
116 folding order from existing literature, termed FP52. Based on experimental evidence,
117 we annotated early folded regions (EFRs) and late folded regions (LFRs) for each
118 protein in FP52, as detailed in Supplementary Text S1 and Table S1.
119

120 Figure 2A illustrates the dynamic evolution of the protein dihedral angles (?, ?) along
121 the PathDiffusion folding trajectory, offering a comprehensive view of how the model
122 explores conformational space and progresses toward the final folded state. Two
123 Ramachandran plots (shown in red) are included as references, depicting the expected
124 dihedral preferences of unfolded and folded states, respectively. The first plot
125 corresponds to unfolded structures and was derived from ~24,000 structures of
126 intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in the human proteome29. The second plot
127 represents folded structures, sourced from 20,000 randomly selected structures in PDB.
128 The blue plots show the dihedral angle distributions from PathDiffusion's folding
129 trajectory, aggregated from conformations at various sampling steps across the 52
130 benchmark proteins. In the initial stages (steps 1-10), the distributions span a broad
131 range of conformational space, largely consistent with highly unfolded starting
132 conformations. As the denoising process advances, the dihedral angles progressively
133 converge and become enriched in regions characteristic of folded structures, such as ß-
134 strands and a-helices, capturing the formation of local secondary structures in
135 intermediate states. At the final stages (steps 290-300), the distributions show strong
136 convergence, closely resembling the patterns observed in PDB structures. This smooth
137 transition in dihedral space demonstrates that PathDiffusion effectively recapitulates
138 the physical folding process, transitioning from a high-entropy disordered state to a
139 low-entropy folded state.
140




141 We further compare PathDiffusion with several leading methods for folding pathway
142 prediction,  including  FoldPAthreader18, AlphaFolding20  and  Pathfinder30.  Both
143 PathDiffusion and FoldPAthreader sample continuous folding trajectory, while
144 AlphaFolding  and  Pathfinder  sample  only  discrete  folding  intermediates.  To
145 concentrate our evaluation on the critical folding process, we filtered the sampled
146 conformations for all methods. For PathDiffusion, we retained 200 intermediate
147 conformations, omitting the initial 50 and the final 50 steps. For FoldPAtheader, we
148 removed conformations from the initialization and finalization stages, retaining only
149 those from the folding nucleation stage. For AlphaFolding and Pathfinder, we used a
150 clustering strategy with a local Distance Difference Test (lDDT) 31 threshold of 0.9 to
151 eliminate redundant structures and select representative conformations.
152

153 The performance of the predicted folding pathway is evaluated using success rate. For
154 each conformation, lDDT relative to the native state is calculated separately for EFRs
155 and LFRs (see Supplementary Text S2). A conformation is classified as a true positive
156 conformation (TPC) if the lDDT of the EFRs is more than 10% higher than that of the
157 LFRs21. A predicted pathway is deemed successful if the TPC ratio (number of TPCs
158 divided by the total conformations) exceeds 0.5 and the TM-score of the final structure
159 surpasses a specified threshold. Finally, the overall success rate is the fraction of
160 proteins in the benchmark dataset with successfully predicted pathways.
161

162 Among the 52 test proteins, PathDiffusion consistently achieves the highest success
163 rate across all TM-score thresholds (Figure 2B). For instance, at a TM-score threshold
164 of 0.5, the success rates are 59.6%, 40.4%, 23.1% and 28.8% for PathDiffusion,
165 FoldPAthreader, AlphaFolding and Pathfinder, respectively. Detailed results in Figure
166 2C and Supplementary Figure S2 show that PathDiffusion not only accurately
167 captures the folding order but also produces high-quality structures, yielding the highest
168 TPC ratio (55.7%) and the highest proportion of near-native structures (90.3% with
169 TM-score = 0.7). This hierarchical folding process is further illustrated in Figure 2D,
170 which displays the trajectory of a representative protein (PDB ID: 1AB7_A), showing




171 that EFRs attain stable structures (higher lDDT) significantly earlier than LFRs. In
172 contrast, FoldPAthreader has a TPC ratio of 39.3% and a near-native rate of 80.7%.
173 Pathfinder generates a substantial fraction of near-native structures (73.1%) but poorly
174 recapitulated folding order (TPC ratio 29.7%). AlphaFolding, limited in landscape
175 exploration, produces the lowest near-native proportion (21.1%). Figure 3 illustrates
176 the predicted folding order for 31 proteins successfully modeled by PathDiffusion (TM-
177 score = 0.5), with structures colored by relative folding timing.

180 PathDiffusion recapitulates molecular dynamics trajectory
181 generated on the Anton supercomputer
182 To evaluate PathDiffusion's ability to capture the equilibrium distribution of folding
183 conformations, we test it on the MD12 dataset, comprising 12 fast-folding proteins that
184 were extensively simulated by MD on the Anton supercomputer32. We compare
185 PathDiffusion against BioEmu using its official model, sampling 300 (BioEmu-3×102)
186 and  10,000  (BioEmu-1×104)  conformations.  For  PathDiffusion,  we  generated
187 continuous folding pathway for each protein comprising 300 conformations, evolving
188 from unfolded to folded states.
189

190 To measure the similarity between sampled and reference MD distributions, we
191 calculated the Jensen-Shannon distance (JSD; range 0-1, lower values indicate greater
192 similarity) across three projected feature dimensions: pairwise distance between ????-
193 carbon atoms (PWD); radius of gyration (RG); and time-lagged independent
194 components (TIC), a low-dimensional representation of dynamics33.
195

196 The quantitative results, presented in Figure 4A, demonstrate that PathDiffusion
197 significantly outperforms BioEmu across all projected dimensions. For PWD,
198 PathDiffusion reduces the average JSD by approximately 28.5% and 24.5% compared
199 to	BioEmu-3×102	and	BioEmu-1×104,	respectively.	Improvements	are	more




200 pronounced for RG (62.2% and 59.2% reductions, respectively), which probes
201 structural compactness, and remains strong for TIC (30.5% and 21.9% reductions). The
202 scatter plots in Figure 4B further confirm this advantage: for most proteins,
203 PathDiffusion JSD values are above the diagonal, indicating closer alignment with
204 ground-truth MD than BioEmu.
205

206 To gain deeper insights into the structural distributions, we visualize the 2D free energy
207 surfaces for the 12 proteins (Figure 4C-E and Supplementary Figure S3). For both
208 Protein G and BBA, the results reveal a similar folding mechanism, characterized by
209 the preferential formation of helical structures followed by the assembly of ß-sheets. In
210 the case of the ????-repressor, the first half of helix 1 along with helices 3 and 4 form
211 preferentially, followed by the assembly of the remaining helices. For Villin, the process
212 exhibits a sequential formation of helices 1-3.
213

214 The free energy surfaces presented in Figure 4D illustrate that the conformations
215 generated by PathDiffusion extensively cover the low-energy basins and transition
216 regions, demonstrating a close overlap with the MD data. In contrast, although BioEmu
217 performs comparably for the protein BBA, its conformations for other proteins are
218 largely restricted to narrow regions near the native state, which limits its ability to
219 adequately sample the intermediate ensembles. This visual analysis is quantitatively
220 supported by the bar charts in Figure 4E, where PathDiffusion yields lower JSD values.
221 This outcome reflects PathDiffusion's superior capability in capturing the distribution
222 of protein folding conformations compared to BioEmu.
223

224 One key advantage of PathDiffusion, beyond accurately estimating conformational
225 distributions, is its ability to generate continuous folding pathways. As illustrated in
226 Supplementary Figure S4, mapping the sampling order of PathDiffusion onto the TIC-
227 projected free energy surface reveals that the generated conformations follow the
228 energy gradient, transitioning smoothly from high-energy unfolded states to low-energy
229 stable states. The intermediate structures generated along this pathway closely align




230 with key structures observed in MD simulations. In contrast, BioEmu is restricted to
231 sampling the final equilibrium or metastable states, lacking the capability to provide
232 insights into the folding trajectory or transition states.

235 PathDiffusion reproduces local folding-unfolding transitions
236 The local folding-unfolding transitions of specific regions plays a key role in biological
237 functions, such as the regulation of signaling switches and autoinhibition mechanisms34,
238 35. PathDiffusion explicitly simulates folding dynamics by sequentially sampling the
239 folding pathway, enabling it to capture locally unfolding states. To assess this capability,
240 we compare PathDiffusion with BioEmu17, DIG14, and AlphaFlow15 on the LT20
241 dataset (derived from the BioEmu study), which includes 20 proteins known to undergo
242 local folding-unfolding transitions. Since local unfolding typically occurs late in
243 folding and/or involves equilibrium fluctuations, we sampled conformations from steps
244 100-300 in the second stage of PathDiffusion. Evaluations were conducted at two
245 sampling scales: a small batch of 200 conformations and a large batch of 10,000
246 conformations.
247

248 We evaluate the quality of the sampled ensembles using the fraction of native contacts
249 (FNC), as defined in the BioEmu study. For a fair comparison, we only consider
250 samples where the non-local regions remain folded (non-local FNC = 0.5). Following
251 the criteria established by BioEmu, a successful sample was defined as having an FNC
252 below 0.3 for the locally unfolded state and above 0.7 for the folded state. We measured
253 the coverage, defined as the percentage of successful conformations in the top 1% of
254 sampled conformations (ranked by local FNC). With a sample size of 200,
255 PathDiffusion achieves a 72.5% recall rate for accurately identifying locally unfolded
256 states, compared with 65%, 37.5%, and 37.5% for BioEmu, DIG, and AlphaFlow,
257 respectively (Figure 5A). While BioEmu has the highest recall for folded states (70%),
258 PathDiffusion remains competitive, correctly identifying 60% of the folded states




259 (Figure 5B). Similar trends are observed when the sample size is increased to 10,000,
260 demonstrating that PathDiffusion can efficiently explore the conformational landscape
261 without requiring massive sampling.
262

263 To validate the biological relevance of PathDiffusion's predictions, we examine three
264 well-characterized proteins known to undergo local folding-unfolding transitions
265 (Figure 5C). The first example is the GAF3 domain of the cyanobacteriochrome
266 Slr1393 (UniProt ID: P73184), a photoreceptor that regulates red/green light switching.
267 Its photocycle involves major structural rearrangement, in which a long disordered loop
268 in the parental state refolds into a short a-helix in the photoproduct state to stabilize the
269 isomerized chromophore. Crystallographic evidence confirms this disorder-to-order
270 transition36. PathDiffusion successfully captures this dynamic feature by generating
271 conformations that represent both flexible loops and folded helices. The second
272 example is the BMP antagonist PRDC (also known as Gremlin-2; UniProt ID: O88273).
273 In the ligand-free state, its N-terminal region of PRDC is intrinsically disordered and
274 highly flexible, enabling it to adopt an ordered conformation upon BMP binding that
275 fills the ligand's binding pocket37. PathDiffusion correctly captures this behavior,
276 producing apo-state conformations with pronounced local flexibility in the N-terminus
277 while maintaining a stable, well-structured core. The third example is human frataxin
278 (UniProt ID: Q16595), a mitochondrial protein whose dysfunction causes Friedreich's
279 ataxia. Experimental studies have linked pathogenicity to increased flexibility of the C-
280 terminal region, which disrupts mitochondrial iron homeostasis38. PathDiffusion
281 sampling consistently reveals elevated local flexibility in this C-terminal segment, in
282 excellent agreement with the experimental observations. Furthermore, visualizations of
283 the predicted results for all 20 proteins are provided in Supplementary Figure S5.
284 These cases collectively demonstrate that PathDiffusion not only generates structurally
285 accurate conformations but also reliably identifies functionally relevant flexibility and
286 folding-unfolding transitions.

289 PathDiffusion	characterizes	conformational	ensembles	of
290 intrinsically disordered proteins
291 Beyond predicting folding pathways for structured proteins, a critical challenge in
292 protein  folding  is  characterizing  the conformational  dynamics  of  intrinsically
293 disordered proteins (IDPs). Because PathDiffusion was trained on both structured and
294 disordered proteins, we applied it to generate ensembles for the dataset IDP50
295 consisting of 50 IDPs. For comparison, we selected 19 structured proteins from the
296 FP52 dataset with matched sequence lengths as controls.
297

298 Analysis of the generated ensembles reveals clear distinctions between structured
299 proteins and IDPs. Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) show that structured proteins
300 rapidly converge to low RMSF values within the first ~100 steps, stabilizing at levels
301 indicative of rigid, native-like states (Figure 6A). In contrast, IDPs sustain markedly
302 higher RMSF throughout the 300-step trajectory, including the final interval (steps 271-
303 300). A parallel pattern emerges in the radius of gyration (Rg, Figure 6B), where IDPs
304 exhibit persistently larger Rg values, consistent with their expanded and heterogeneous
305 conformational ensembles.
306

307 To probe the structural origins of this flexibility, we examined the correlation between
308 per-residue contact degree (based on native contacts) and intra-ensemble lDDT scores
309 (Figure 6C). Residues with high contact degrees achieve higher lDDT values,
310 indicating greater structural stability, while those with few contacts show lower lDDT,
311 reflecting	elevated	flexibility.	This	demonstrates	that	PathDiffusion	reliably
312 distinguishes locally ordered motifs from fully disordered regions. We conducted an
313 analysis of an example intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) that has experimentally
314 resolved NMR ensembles in PDB (Figure 6D, E). It is an Arabidopsis thaliana RALF8
315 peptide (PDB ID: 6NU4), which is primarily disordered but features a short segment
316 that is locally ordered (residues 20-30). PathDiffusion effectively identifies this
317 structured segment while accurately capturing the overall disordered behavior of the




318 peptide. These results highlight PathDiffusion's robustness in modeling both stable
319 structural elements and flexible regions, facilitating the exploration of complex and
320 heterogeneous conformational landscapes.

323 PathDiffusion resolves distinct folding mechanisms in TIM-
324 barrel proteins
325 Although TIM-barrel proteins are characterized by a highly conserved topology,
326 featuring eight parallel ß-strands surrounded by eight a-helices, they exhibit remarkably
327 distinct folding mechanisms. To evaluate whether PathDiffusion can capture these
328 variations in folding pathways, we analyzed three representative TIM-barrel proteins:
329 indole-3-glycerol phosphate synthase (sIGPS, PDB ID: 2C3Z)39, a-subunit of
330 tryptophan synthase (aTS, PDB ID: 1BKS)40, and rabbit muscle aldolase (Aldolase,
331 PDB ID: 1ADO)41. These proteins exhibit high structural similarity (average TM-score
332 0.63) but low sequence identity (average 0.19), and their folding intermediates have
333 been extensively characterized by hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry
334 (HX-MS).
335

336 For sIGPS, the folding trajectory simulated by PathDiffusion is highly consistent with
337 the "center-first" folding mechanism revealed by experimental data (Figure 7A). HX-
338 MS studies have identified a stable equilibrium intermediate comprising the central
339 regions (ßa)2-5ß6, whereas the N- and C-terminal regions remain largely unprotected
340 and disordered39. Accordingly, the central segment (residues 50-160) is classified as the
341 EFRs, while the two ends are LFRs. Consistent with this mechanism, PathDiffusion
342 demonstrates a clear hierarchical folding where the lDDT of the EFRs increases
343 significantly faster than that of the LFRs. Structurally, the central ß barrel forms a stable
344 core by step 93, whereas the N- and C-termini remain disordered until after step 205.
345 This observation was confirmed by quantitative Z-score analysis (Supplementary Text
346 S3), where the PathDiffusion produces high scores (indicating early folding) for the




347 central residues (50-160), almost identical to the high urea protection profile of the
348 native structure.
349

350 For aTS, experimental data indicates that the folding intermediate resides in the N-
351 terminal submodule40. PathDiffusion recapitulates this this N-terminal preference:
352 lDDT and secondary structure analyses show that the N-terminal domain forms earlier
353 than the C-terminus (Figure 7B). Visual inspection of the trajectory reveals that the N-
354 terminal region has formed a stable region at step 86, while the C-terminal region
355 gradually folds in subsequent steps. This asymmetry is further corroborated by the Z-
356 score profile, which shows positive scores at the N-terminus (residues 12-130).
357 Together, these results highlight PathDiffusion's sensitivity to sequence-specific
358 stability determinants, rather than merely memorizing a generic barrel topology.
359

360 For Aldolase, the folding mechanism is notably more complex. Experimental dialysis
361 studies categorize the protein backbone into three stability levels based on their folding
362 rates41. Accordingly, we designated regions with the slowest refolding rates (residues
363 58-130 and 300-363) as LFRs and those with fast or medium rates as EFRs. The
364 PathDiffusion generated trajectory reveals that the EFRs begin to form around step 101,
365 subsequently integrating with other regions (Figure 7C). Overall, the predicted folding
366 hierarchy aligns closely with the experimental refolding rates, with one key exception
367 at the N-terminus (residues 1-17). PathDiffusion assigns lower Z-scores (indicating
368 later folding) than observed experimentally. This discrepancy likely stems from
369 quaternary interactions that stabilize the N-terminus in the native homotetramer, which
370 are absent in our monomeric simulations.
371

372 In conclusion, although the three TIM-barrel proteins follow different folding pathways,
373 they exhibit common features. All TIM-barrel proteins appear to form a small ß-a-ß-a
374 module in the early stages. This module provides a basic scaffold, to which other
375 secondary structural elements are added sequentially, ultimately yielding one or more
376 stable intermediates. PathDiffusion successfully detects these early stable regions and




377 predicts their folding order, providing mechanistic insights into how TIM-barrel
378 proteins navigate their complex folding energy landscapes.

381 Ablation studies
382 To quantify the contributions of two key data-driven components (evolution-informed
383 noise schedule and sequence-conditional score model) in PathDiffusion, we conduct
384 ablation studies (Figure 8A). The default PathDiffusion achieves a baseline success
385 rate of 59.6% at a TPC threshold of 0.5. When both the evolution-informed noise
386 schedule and the score model are removed, the performance drop to the lowest level,
387 with a success rate of only 11.5%.
388

389 Evolution-informed noise schedule. We investigate the impact of the evolution-
390 informed noise schedule (i.e., PSNS). We first replace PSNS by random noise schedule.
391 In this setting, the target sequence is segmented based on predicted secondary structures,
392 and random noise schedules are assigned to the residues within each segment. This
393 leads to a sharp decline in success rate from 59.6% to 28.8%. To test if a general
394 physical prior could bridge this gap, we then implement a rule-based schedule. We
395 introduce a rule-based noise schedule that imposes sequential ordering from N- to C-
396 terminus and a structural hierarchy assigning progressively lower noise to a-helices, ß-
397 sheets, and loops, respectively. Although this rule-based schedule outperforms the
398 random schedule at lower TPC thresholds, it yielded only comparable results at higher
399 thresholds, and both strategies falling far short of the full model. This result highlights
400 that general physical rules cannot substitute for the position-specific evolutionary signal
401 encoded in PSNS. The PSNS, derived from multiple structure alignment, is essential
402 for guiding PathDiffusion toward the correct folding pathway of the target protein.
403

404 Sequence-conditional score model. We next ablate the sequence-conditional score
405 network by replacing its predicted scores with scores computed directly from the static




406 ground-truth structure (see Section 2.3). This change reduces the success rate to 34.6%
407 at a TPC threshold of 0.5, indicating that the learned score model captures evolutionary
408 information about folding dynamics that is superior to static geometric priors derived
409 solely from the native state.
410

411 Examination of TPC distributions for models relying on only one component further
412 confirms that that the two components are complementary (Figure 8B), thus combining
413 both sources yields markedly superior pathway reconstruction compared to either alone.
414 These ablation experiments demonstrate that PathDiffusion's strong performance arises
415 from the tight synergy between the evolution-informed noise schedule and the
416 sequence-conditioned score model.
417

418 Generative capabilities of the score models. We further evaluate the generative
419 capabilities of the sequence-conditional score model and the unconditional score model.
420 Both models are trained to convergence, using 100 denoising steps for structure
421 generation. For the sequence-conditional score model, we utilized a uniform noise
422 schedule (applying the same noise level to all residues) to generate 100 structures for
423 each target. As shown in Figure 8C, the average RMSF for EFRs and LFRs were 1.205
424 Å and 1.676 Å, respectively, with 81% of targets exhibiting higher RMSF in LFRs than
425 in EFRs. This indicates that the sequence-conditional model effectively captures
426 intrinsic	structural	flexibility,	revealing	that	LFRs	typically	exhibit	greater
427 conformational diversity than EFRs during functional dynamics with representative
428 ensembles shown in Figure 8E. For the unconditional score model, we generate one
429 conformation per target and calculated the radius of gyration (Rg) (Figure 8D). The
430 resulting Rg values increase with protein length in close agreement with the statistical
431 scaling laws observed for intrinsically disordered proteins42, 43. This suggests that the
432 unconditional model has successfully learned the generic physical properties and spatial
433 scaling constraints that govern expanded and disordered conformations.

436 Analysis of compactional efficiency
437 To evaluate the practical utility of PathDiffusion, we benchmarked its computational
438 efficiency. All experiments were conducted locally on an A100 GPU with 40 GB of
439 memory. We measured the runtime required to sample folded trajectory on the FP52
440 dataset using the default parameters for each method. As illustrated in Supplementary
441 Figure S6A, PathDiffusion is slower than AlphaFolding but 5-10 times faster than both
442 FoldPAthreader and Pathfinder.
443

444 We further assessed the computational cost of generating extensive conformational
445 ensembles for 12 fast-folding proteins (Supplementary Figure S6B). PathDiffusion
446 samples 10,000 conformations in under 3 hours. Although slower than BioEmu (< l.5
447 hours) due to its more extensive denoising schedule (100 steps versus 30-50 for
448 BioEmu), both methods operate on comparable timescales suitable for practical use. In
449 contrast, previous studies have reported that all-atom MD simulations require 2,000-
450 100,000 GPU hours to produce equivalent ensembles17. Consequently, PathDiffusion
451 achieves an acceleration of four to five orders of magnitude compared to traditional
452 MD	simulations.	In	summary,	PathDiffusion	effectively	recovers	protein
453 conformational ensembles and elucidates folding pathways with high accuracy, offering
454 substantial potential for investigating protein folding pathway and dynamics.

457	Conclusions
458 In this study, we introduce PathDiffusion, a generative diffusion framework that
459 directly predicts continuous protein folding pathway from amino acid sequence. By
460 incorporating residue-level evolutionary information into a two-stage diffusion process,
461 PathDiffusion efficiently explores protein folding pathway while preserving high
462 native-state accuracy. Unlike existing methods that primarily sample near-native
463 equilibrium distributions, PathDiffusion explicitly simulates the dynamic process of
464 folding. It allocates more sampling resources to exploring unfolded states and transition




465 regions as well as nucleation events, making the method more suitable for studying
466 folding mechanisms that evolve over time.
467

468 PathDiffusion does not rely on MD simulations data for training or fine-tuning. It
469 predicts folding pathways in a zero-shot manner, achieving several orders of magnitude
470 speed	improvement	while	maintaining	strong	agreement	with	MD-derived
471 conformational ensembles. Compared to existing deep learning-based ensemble
472 generators, PathDiffusion generates a richer variety of intermediates and can
473 distinguish different folding mechanisms even among topologically similar proteins.
474 PathDiffusion can also reproduce function-related local unfolding-folding transition
475 events, which are crucial for allosteric regulation and misfolding-related diseases.
476 Furthermore, extensive evaluations on intrinsically disordered proteins demonstrate
477 that PathDiffusion effectively reproduces the intrinsic structural flexibility and
478 conformational heterogeneity of disordered regions. Collectively, these capabilities
479 position PathDiffusion as a powerful bridge linking sequence information to structural
480 dynamics and biological function.
481

482 Despite these advances, limitations remain. The current framework relies on MSA and
483 homologous structures to estimate the evolutionary information, reducing accuracy for
484 orphan proteins or de novo designs lacking evolutionary context. Future work could
485 develop single-sequence dynamical inference from predicted features44. Additionally,
486 diffusion steps represent abstract progression rather than physical time, precluding
487 direct prediction of absolute folding rates or metastable state lifetimes. Future
488 extensions could incorporate biophysical principles, such as coarse-grained energy
489 estimation or kinetic network models, to map generated pathways onto a physically-
490 based free energy landscape and provide approximate kinetic information45, 46.

493	Methods
494 1. Training and test data
495 Training datasets
496 PathDiffusion employs two specialized diffusion models: a sequence-conditional
497 diffusion model for folding structured regions and a conditional diffusion model for
498 handling unstructured (intrinsically disordered) regions. To train these models, we
499 constructed two dedicated datasets. The structured dataset consists of ~530,000
500 structures from PDB and the unstructured dataset contains ~24,000 intrinsically
501 disordered	regions	from	CALVADOS	coarse-grained	simulations29.	Detailed
502 procedures for dataset construction, including filtering criteria and processing steps, are
503 provided in the Supplementary Information.

504

505 Test datasets
506 PathDiffusion are evaluated on four independent benchmark datasets that cover diverse
507 aspects of protein folding dynamics, which are briefly introduced below. FP52: 52
508 proteins with experimentally determined folding pathways, curated from the literature;
509 MD12: 12 fast-folding proteins with long-timescale molecular dynamics trajectory
510 simulated on the Anton supercomputer32; LT20: 20 proteins exhibiting local folding-
511 unfolding transitions, selected from the BioEmu study17; IDP50: 50 intrinsically
512 disordered proteins sourced from PDB. Additional details on dataset composition,
513 selection criteria, and preprocessing are available in the Supplementary Information.

516 2. PathDiffusion algorithm
517 PathDiffusion works through two major modules (Figure 1), where the first module
518 prepares a position-specific noise schedules (PSNS) from evolutionary information and
519 the second module iteratively generate folding pathway using PSNS-guided two-stage
520 diffusion models.

720 2.6 Model implementation and training
721 PathDiffusion trains two complementary diffusion models: a sequence-conditional
722 model and an unconditional model. The sequence-conditional diffusion model is trained
723 on a clustered version of the PDB database, enabling it to generate structured
724 conformations guided by the input amino acid sequence. In contrast, the unconditional
725 model is trained on conformational ensembles of human intrinsically disordered regions
726 generated through coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations from CALVADOS29.
727 This specialized training allows the unconditional model to capture the characteristic
728 flexibility and dynamical behavior of disordered segments.

729

730 Input representations
731 For the sequence-conditional model, the input representations are designed to integrate
732 evolutionary and structural signals (Figure 1D). The single representation is
733 constructed by concatenating amino acid sequence embeddings with PSNS-based
734 diffusion time embeddings, and ESMFold-derived node embeddings. Pairwise
735 representations are constructed by concatenating sequence embeddings, PSNS-based
736 diffusion time embeddings, inter-residue distances computed from the noisy frame, and




737 ESMFold-derived edge embeddings.
738

739 For the unconditional model, the input features are adjusted to avoid biasing the model
740 towards precomputed structural priors. The single representation is constructed by
741 concatenating a masked residue position encoding (hiding amino acid identity) with a
742 global time embedding, where the PSNS is replaced with a uniform noise schedule.
743 Similarly, pairwise representations are constructed by concatenating residue position
744 encodings, a global time embedding, and inter-residue distances.
745

746 Structure Module
747 Following the ConfDiff48 framework, our structure module adapts the AlphaFold250
748 architecture to a frame-based system. It integrates invariant point attention (IPA) layers,
749 transformer layers, and multilayer perceptron (MLP) to generate and refine three-
750 dimensional conformations. The network architecture is shown in Supplementary
751 Figure S1.
752

753 The IPA module comprises 6 layers, with a single representation dimensionality of 256,
754 a pair representation dimensionality of 128, and a hidden dimensionality of 256. Each
755 IPA layer employs 6 attention heads, 8 query points, and 12 value points. The
756 Transformer module contains 2 layers, each with 8 attention heads. Finally, multi-head
757 attention and MLP layers are used for edge feature transitions and torsion angle updates,
758 enabling the accurate prediction of folded protein conformations.

910 framework consists of two modules. Module 1 derives position-specific noise schedules
911 (PSNS) from evolutionary information encoded in multiple sequence alignment (MSA)
912 and multiple structure alignment (MSTA). Module 2 simulates the folding pathway
913 using an evolution-guided, two-stage diffusion strategy. In Stage 1 a sequence-
914 conditional diffusion model is used to generate an initial folding trajectory. In Stage 2,
915 trajectory dynamics are refined via an adaptive dual-score fusion mechanism. (B)
916 Adaptive dual-score fusion mechanism in Stage 2. During denoising, the model
917 combines two score estimators: a sequence-conditional score (directing toward the
918 native  folded  state)  and  an  unconditional  score  (directing  toward  unfolded
919 conformations). These are integrated via a residue-specific adaptive weight w to
920 produce the final predicted score. (C) Evolution-guided diffusion process. Forward
921 noising and reverse denoising steps are modulated by PSNS, enabling structurally
922 heterogeneous folding rates: evolutionarily conserved regions denoise (structure)
923 earlier and more robustly, while variable regions remain flexible/noisy longer,
924 reflecting biophysical folding principles. (D) Single-step denoising architecture. The
925 model accepts a noisy conformation (and optional sequence) as input. This state,

Two training datasets were constructed accordingly1019to train both models.10201021Training dataset of structured proteins. The training and validation datasets were1022constructed using single-chain structures from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) to train the1023sequence-conditional diffusion model. We used entries released prior to April 10, 2024,1024for training, and those deposited between April 10, 2024, and April 16, 2025, for1025validation. Following the data processing strategy proposed in ConfDiff1, we expanded1026our dataset by extracting and preserving all available structural models within each1027PDB entry, which allows us to fully leverage the conformational ensembles provided1028by experimental techniques such as solution NMR. This screening process yielded 681 candidate proteins, from which 50 were randomly selected.

Text S3. Z-score analysis of folding order
To quantitatively validate the predicted folding pathways against experimental evidence, we performed a comparative Z-score analysis. The experimental data were derived from HX-MS datasets reported in the literature, utilizing specific metrics appropriate for each protein's experimental design:
* sIGPS (PDB: 2C3Z): Data were obtained from equilibrium pulse-labeling experiments in urea6. The metric used was the percentage of protected amide protons within the stable equilibrium intermediate populated at ~5 M urea. Higher protection levels indicate regions that form the stable structural core.
* ????TS (PDB: 1BKS): Data were derived from kinetic refolding pulse-labeling experiments7. The metric used was the relative population of protected species for each peptide segment, measured after 2 seconds of refolding. A higher population indicates regions that fold rapidly into the kinetic intermediate.
* Aldolase (PDB: 1ADO): Data were collected during refolding via dialysis8. The metric used was the inverse of the dialysis time (1/????1/2) required for a
segment to achieve protection. Since earlier protection occurs at higher denaturant concentrations (shorter dialysis times), the inverse time serves as a proxy for folding priority.

For the PathDiffusion predictions, we quantified the folding timing for each residue by analyzing the generated trajectories. All sample conformations were globally aligned to the final state. We calculated a folding proportion for each residue, defined as the percentage of conformations in the trajectory where the C???? atomic distance to the corresponding residue of final state was less than 3 Å.
