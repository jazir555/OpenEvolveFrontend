009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011
               Multi-agent systems (MAS) based on Large Language Models (LLMs) have the
012
               potential to solve tasks that are beyond the reach of any single LLM. However,
013
               this potential can only be realized when the collaboration mechanism between
014            agents is optimized. Specifically, optimizing the communication structure be-
015            tween agents is critical for fruitful collaboration. Most existing approaches rely on
016            fixed topologies, pretrained graph generators, optimization over edges, or employ
017            external LLM judges, thereby adding to the complexity. In this work, we introduce
018            a response-conditioned framework that adapts communication on-the-fly. Agents
019            independently generate responses to the user query and assess peer contributions
020            using an approximation of the Shapley value. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is
021            then constructed to regulate the propagation of the responses among agents, which
022
               ensures stable and efficient message transmission from high-contributing agents
               to others. This graph is dynamically updated based on the agent responses from
023
               the previous collaboration round. Since the proposed framework enables the self-
024
               organization of agents without additional supervision or training, we refer to it
025            as S ELF O RG. The S ELF O RG framework goes beyond task- and query-level opti-
026            mization and takes into account the stochastic nature of agent responses. Experi-
027            ments with both strong and weak LLM backends demonstrate robust performance,
028            with significant gains in the weak regime where prior methods collapse. We also
029            theoretically show that multiple agents increase the chance of correctness and that
030            the correct responses naturally dominate the information flow.

      1   I NTRODUCTION
035
036   Large Language Models (LLMs) (OpenAI, 2023; Dubey et al., 2024; Anthropic, 2025; Qwen et al.,
037   2025) have rapidly advanced capabilities across planning, analysis, coding, and dialog, yet a single
038   LLM still faces notable limitations: stochastic or unreliable generations, hallucinations, and diffi-
039
      culty with long-horizon, multi-step tasks. A natural response has been to move from a solitary model
      to a multi-agent system (MAS) of LLMs, where agents interact, critique, and refine one another’s
040
      outputs (Li et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Zhuge et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024b; Ye et al., 2025a).
041
      In principle, this collective can surpass an individual model by pooling complementary reasoning
042   paths; in practice, however, the gains depend critically on how the agents are orchestrated: who
043   communicates with whom, when, and how final outputs are aggregated.
044
045
      Prior work has explored a spectrum of communication topologies. Fixed structures include chains,
      trees, complete graphs, and random graphs; scalable studies compare these patterns across task
046
      families such as mathematical reasoning, knowledge reasoning, and coding (Qian et al., 2025).
047
      Beyond static designs, some approaches treat the topology as optimizable: edges are sampled and
048   trained with policy gradients or masks (e.g., GPTSwarm (Zhuge et al., 2024), AgentPrune (Zhang
049   et al., 2025a)). A complementary line delegates topology design to a separate model that outputs
050   a task/query-specific communication graph (e.g., G-Designer (Zhang et al., 2025b), MAS-GPT (Ye
051   et al., 2025b). Others rely on an external LLM “judge” to rank, filter, or make final decisions
052   (Ebrahimi et al., 2025). While effective in certain settings, these strategies introduce substantial
053   overhead: pretraining a graph generator; reinforcement learning over edges; repeated calls to a
      judge LLM.

071   A common hypothesis in this literature is that there exists a “best” topology per task category (e.g.,
072   math vs. coding). This idea has evolved toward finer granularity, that the query should determine
073   the topology (one graph per problem). We argue that both views are ultimately brittle. Because
074   LLM agents are inherently stochastic, the information that matters for coordination is not the static
075   task label nor the problem identity, but the state the agents are actually in – their concrete responses
076   at a given time step. Two agents may answer the same query differently across runs; a topology
077   that was ideal yesterday may be suboptimal today. Thus, the communication pattern should be
078   decided on the fly, conditioned on the current pool of responses. Searching for a universally superior
079
      topology per task or per query is therefore potentially confounded and fragile: it risks overfitting to
      incidental response patterns or to powerful base models whose single-shot accuracy already masks
080
      orchestration weaknesses.
081
082   This state-driven perspective is especially revealing in the weak-backend regime, where each agent
083   has a modest chance of being correct. In such settings, the value of orchestration should be to
084   amplify rare correct responses and suppress noise, not to lean on an already-competent model.
085
      Our approach embraces this principle: we propose a decentralized, response-conditioned framework
      in which agents (i) independently produce initial answers, (ii) locally assess peers via a Shapley
086
      value-inspired contribution valuation, and (iii) construct a directed acyclic communication graph
087
      (DAG) that routes information from high-contribution agents to others. This yields a lightweight
088   system with no external judge, no pretrained topology generator, and no edge-level reinforcement
089   learning, yet it adapts its structure per instance.
090
091
      We make the following contributions:
092         1. We construct a per-instance DAG directly from agents’ current responses via semantic
093            alignment, avoiding fixed topologies, pretrained graph generators, and edge-level RL.
094
            2. We quantify influence with a Shapley-inspired utility, together with efficient approximation
095
               and ranking-stability guarantees, enabling lightweight, model-agnostic credit assignment.
096
097
            3. We analyze why multi-agent interaction amplifies correct signals and why correct respon-
               ders dominate contributions, and we validate S ELF O RG across various reasoning bench-
098
               marks and multiple backbones.

101   2   M ETHODOLOGY
102
103   We propose a multi-agent collaborative framework that adaptively constructs its communication
104   structure without relying on external judges, pretrained graph generators, or reinforcement learning
105   for edge optimization. The key principle is to leverage agents’ own responses to estimate their con-
106   tributions, estimate these contributions using Shapley values, and enforce a directed acyclic com-
107   munication graph (DAG) for stable information propagation. In what follows, we describe each
      component in detail. The overall pipeline of S ELF O RG is illustrated in Figure 1.

269   We now provide a probabilistic perspective to explain why our framework amplifies correct re-
      sponses, particularly when the underlying LLMs are weak. The following analysis highlights two

      complementary mechanisms: (i) with multiple agents, the probability that at least two agents are
271   correct grows rapidly with N ; and (ii) whenever multiple agents agree on the same response, that
272   response is overwhelmingly likely to be correct. Together, these principles explain why correctness
273   not only appears more often in multi-agent settings but also dominates the contribution scores.
274

275
      sistently appears across 100 runs of Qwen-1.5B, wrong answers are scattered with little overlap.
276

277   answers form a tight cluster, whereas incorrect ones are scattered. Finally, an intervention study
278   shows that when an agent receives input from the top-contributor, its probability of solving the task
279   rises from 49% to 69%. These findings motivate the need for contribution estimation and leader
280   selection in S ELF O RG.
281

440   4   R ELATED W ORK
441
442   Multi-Agent Systems. Early multi-agent systems such as CAMEL (Li et al., 2023) and AutoGen
443   (Wu et al., 2024) introduced role-based LLM agents that collaborate through dialogue. Debate-style
444   systems encourage adversarial or diverse reasoning to refine answers (Du et al., 2023; Liang et al.,
445   2024; Subramaniam et al., 2025), while dynamic orchestration (AgentVerse (Chen et al., 2024),
446
      DyLAN (Liu et al., 2024)) adapts team composition or roles during execution. More recent efforts
      aim for automatic workflow generation (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025c;b; Ye et al., 2025b),
447
      though these rely on strong meta-agents or pretrained generators, adding overhead and limiting
448
      autonomy. Multi-agent collaboration has also been applied to diverse domains including software
449   (Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024a), recommendation (Zhang et al., 2024), medicine (Tang et al.,
450   2024), finance (Li et al., 2024), education (Zhang et al., 2025e), and science (Zeng et al., 2024).
451
452
      Communication Graphs. Prior work has explored a spectrum of communication topologies. Fixed
      structures include chains, trees, complete graphs, and random graphs, with recent studies system-
453
      atically comparing these patterns across task families such as mathematical reasoning, knowledge
454
      reasoning, and coding (Qian et al., 2025). Beyond static designs, some approaches treat the topol-
455   ogy as optimizable: edges are sampled and trained with policy gradients or masks (Zhuge et al.,
456   2024; Zhang et al., 2025a; Qian et al., 2025). A complementary line delegates topology design to a
457   separate model that outputs a task- or query-specific communication graph (Zhang et al., 2025b; Ye
458   et al., 2025b). Other frameworks rely on an external LLM “judge” to rank, filter, or finalize outputs
459   (Liu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025c; Zhuge et al., 2025; Ebrahimi et al., 2025). While effective
460   in constrained settings, these strategies incur substantial overhead: pretraining graph generators,
461   optimization over edges, or repeated calls to a judge LLM.
462   These approaches assume that an optimal or near-optimal graph exists either per task category or
463   even per query. However, such assumptions can be misleading: because LLM agents are stochastic,
464   the same agent may succeed on one query and fail on another. Our method instead constructs the
465   graph on-the-fly, adapting dynamically to the actual responses produced.
466
      Contribution Assessment in Collaborative Systems. Numerous systems in LLM-based MAS as-
467   sess agent quality with additional LLMs. For instance, LLM-Blender (Jiang et al., 2023b) uses an
468   additional LLM for pairwise comparisons, incurring O(N 2 ) operations for N agents, while DyLAN
469   (Liu et al., 2024) introduces a dedicated LLM agent to score responses; other MAS frameworks
470   similarly rely on judge models to value and select contributions (Ebrahimi et al., 2025). Outside
471   multi-agent systems, the broader literature on contribution valuation offers principled tools originat-
472   ing from cooperative game theory (Shapley, 1953), with concrete instantiations in federated learning
473   (McMahan et al., 2017; Jia et al., 2019). FL works measure participant contributions via Shapley
474   values (Jia et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Tastan et al., 2024), influence functions
475
      (Rokvic et al., 2024), self-reported information (Kang et al., 2019), and utility-game formulations
      (Wang et al., 2019). We draw a direct parallel to MAS and instantiate Shapley-style contribution es-
476
      timates over agent responses (Section 2.3), eliminating external judges and additional training while
477
      maintaining principled contribution estimation.

480   5   C ONCLUSION
481
482   We presented S ELF O RG, a framework for orchestrating LLM-based multi-agent systems without ex-
483   ternal pretrained topology generators or reinforcement learning. By leveraging response-conditioned
484
      contribution estimation and adaptive graph formation, S ELF O RG amplifies correct signals and sup-
      presses noise. Our theoretical analysis and empirical results across diverse reasoning benchmarks
485
      confirm that it consistently outperforms prior orchestration baselines.

919
920   Baselines.   We use the benchmark authors’ implementations where available (Ye et al., 2025a).
921
922         • MacNet (Qian et al., 2025) is run with 5 agents and the random topology, following the
923           paper’s strongest reported configuration.
924         • DyLAN (Liu et al., 2024) uses 4 agents and 3 rounds.
925
            • AgentVerse (Chen et al., 2024) and AutoGen (Wu et al., 2024) are run with their public
926           defaults adapted to the benchmark.
927
            • G-Designer (Zhang et al., 2025b) is evaluated on Qwen-2.5-1.5B-Instruct; we omit larger
928
              models because it requires training a separate graph generator, and thus latency-inefficient
929           (see Sections 1 and 4 for discussion).
930
931            We include G-Designer (Zhang et al., 2025b) in our Qwen-1.5B experiments, as it is
932            among the most closely related graph-optimizing methods. However, its design differs
933            fundamentally from S ELF O RG. G-Designer trains a separate graph generator that outputs
934            a communication topology conditioned on the query and predefined agent roles. While this
935            is effective with stronger backbones, it does not adapt to the responses actually produced
936            by weak agents, which are often noisy. As a result, its learned graphs fail to amplify correct
937            signals in the low-capacity regime, leading to poor empirical performance (see Table 1).
938
939           For larger models, we do not run G-Designer, since it requires training a dedicated
940           graph generator. This introduces substantial overhead and deviates from our goal of ef-
941
              ficient, judge-free orchestration. Our design philosophy emphasizes lightweight, response-
              conditioned self-organization without external generators or meta-agents, as discussed in
942
              Sections 1 and 4.
943
944
            • To compare with single agent execution methods, we incorporate evaluations against sin-
              gle execution and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022).
945
946
      S ELF O RG configuration.     S ELF O RG is configured as:
947
948         • Agent pool:       {Assistant, Programmer, Mathematician, Economist,
949           Psychologist, Historian, Lawyer, Doctor}.
950         • Number of agents: for math-based tasks: 4 agents with fixed roles (from the pool), and
951           for science and knowledge: 5 agents up to psychologist.
952
            • Neighbor selection: top-2 neighbors per agent (by pairwise cosine similarity S); similarity
953
              threshold τ = 0.5 for edge formation.
954
            • Rounds and structure: maximum of 3 rounds (including decentralized initialization);
955
              with DAG enforcement.
956
957         • Contribution estimation: we use all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embedding model with embed-
958
              ding dimension of 384 (lightweight sentence embedding model).
959         • Aggregation: contribution-weighted centroid (Equation 6); final answer is the nearest re-
960           sponse to the centroid.
961         • Reform policy: we reform the DAG in each round from updated responses.
962
963   Agent Profiling. We adopt a standard community template for defining agent roles, widely used
964   in prior multi-agent system benchmarks. In our experiments, a subset of four/five agents is instan-
965   tiated per run (default), selected in fixed order unless otherwise specified. Each role is assigned a
966
      default prompt template (system instruction) from the benchmark community, without additional
      fine-tuning or hand-engineering. This ensures that performance differences arise from orchestration
967
      rather than custom role design.

1170      D.3                     E FFICIENT S ELF O RG
1171
1172     While the main pipeline of S ELF O RG proceeds through multiple rounds, not all rounds are equally
1173     necessary. In practice, if the agents already achieve strong agreement, further refinement may waste
1174
         tokens without improving accuracy. To address this, we introduce an early-stopping mechanism
         based on natural consensus among peers.
1175
1176
1177     Consensus Criterion. Let the similarity matrix S ∈ [−1, 1] be defined as in Section 2.4, where
1178
         Si,j = cos (rn , rm ) encodes the pairwise agreement between agents i and j. We define the minimum
         consensus across all pairs as Smin = mini̸=j Si,j . Intuitively, Smin captures the weakest agreement
1179
         within the system. If this minimum exceeds a predefined threshold γ ∈ [0, 1], then the agents are
1180
         deemed to have reached sufficient consensus.
1181
1182     Formally, the system halts further rounds if Smin ≥ γ, where γ is the consensus parameter con-
1183     trolling strictness of agreement. For example, γ = 0.9 requires that all pairs of responses have at
1184
         least 90% cosine similarity. When satisfied, the system outputs the centroid-based final response
         (Equation 6) without additional rounds.
1185
1186     This mechanism directly builds upon the communication graph formation step (Section 2.4). Since
1187     embeddings and similarities are already computed, evaluating Smin incurs negligible overhead. By
         stopping once consensus is achieved, S ELF O RG avoids redundant propagation and aggregation,

1220     compared to the baseline. The stricter γ = 0.95 yields moderate savings, while the looser γ = 0.9

1222     remains stable, with savings on the order of 10 − 15% across benchmarks.
1223
1224     Summary. Efficient S ELF O RG demonstrates that natural peer consensus can serve as a reliable
1225     early-stopping signal. By halting once strong agreement is reached, the system avoids redundant
1226     message-passing rounds, improving token efficiency while preserving accuracy. Unlike prior MAS
1227     approaches such as DyLAN, which require explicit answer extraction from responses to measure
1228
         consensus (and may fail if the LLM deviates from formatting instructions), our method operates
         purely in the embedding space and thus avoids brittle dependencies on response parsing. Similarly,
1229
         works that rely on external LLM judges to check consensus introduce additional computational and
1230
         monetary overhead. In contrast, Efficient S ELF O RG is lightweight, model-agnostic, and robust: no
1231     answer extraction is needed, no external judge is invoked, and consensus is measured semantically
1232     rather than syntactically. This makes it especially suitable for scaling to large agent pools and diverse
1233     task domains.
