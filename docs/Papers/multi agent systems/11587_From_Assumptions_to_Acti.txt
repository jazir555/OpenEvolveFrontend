009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011            Embodied agents operating in multi-agent, partially observable, and decentralized
012
               environments must plan and act despite pervasive uncertainty about hidden objects
               and collaborators’ intentions. Recent advances in applying Large Language Mod-
013
               els (LLMs) to embodied agents have addressed many long-standing challenges,
014
               such as high-level goal decomposition and online adaptation. Yet, uncertainty is
015            still primarily mitigated through frequent inter-agent communication. This incurs
016            substantial token and time costs, and can disrupt established workflows, when hu-
017            man partners are involved. We introduce PCE, a Planner-Composer-Evaluator
018            framework that converts the fragmented assumptions latent in LLM reasoning
019            traces into a structured decision tree. Internal nodes encode environment assump-
020            tions and leaves map to actions; each path is then scored by scenario likelihood,
021            goal-directed gain, and execution cost to guide rational action selection without
022            heavy communication. Across two challenging multi-agent benchmarks (C-WAH
023
               and TDW-MAT) and three diverse LLM backbones, PCE consistently outperforms
               communication-centric baselines in success rate and task efficiency while show-
024
               ing comparable token usage. Ablation results indicate that the performance gains
025
               obtained by scaling model capacity or reasoning depth persist even when PCE
026            is applied, while PCE consistently raises the baseline across both capacity and
027            reasoning-depth scales, confirming that structured uncertainty handling comple-
028            ments both forms of scaling. A user study further demonstrates that PCE produces
029            communication patterns that human partners perceive as more efficient and trust-
030            worthy. Together, these results establish a principled route for turning latent LLM
031            assumptions into reliable strategies for uncertainty-aware planning.

034   1   I NTRODUCTION
035
036   Imagine two household agents collaborating to prepare a meal: each perceives only part of the
037   kitchen, yet both must coordinate to assemble ingredients and deliver them to a shared workspace.
038   Such agents exemplify embodied agents, which perceive their surroundings, formulate plans, and
039
      execute actions to achieve specified goals in dynamic environments (Fung et al., 2025). When mul-
      tiple embodied agents must cooperate in a decentralized setting under partial observability, each
040
      agent’s limited perceptual field produces pervasive uncertainties about unobserved objects as well
041
      as the intentions and actions of collaborators (Spaan et al., 2006; Amato et al., 2016; 2015). Naively
042   exploring these uncertainties at every planning step is computationally prohibitive. It also tends to
043   produce suboptimal or inconsistent plans, as the combinatorial growth of possible hidden states sig-
044   nificantly increases the difficulty of assessing the relative value of available actions and maintaining
045   a coherent belief.
046
      Agents powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved notable progress in such set-
047
      tings. By leveraging few-shot and zero-shot reasoning, LLM-based planners can decompose high-
048   level goals into executable action sequences, adapt plans to environmental changes, and reliably
049   infer the intentions of the collaborators, enabling robust performance in complex long-horizon set-
050   tings. To handle uncertainty, however, most existing systems rely on repeated natural-language
051   communication with collaborators to verify plans, exchange information, and iteratively refine joint
052   strategies. This communication-centric paradigm incurs significant costs: frequent dialogue con-
053   sumes large numbers of tokens and time, and when human collaborators are involved, continuous
      questioning and reporting can disrupt established workflows. Moreover, simply increasing LLM


                                                         1
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




054
      capacity or deepening its reasoning chains does not inherently resolve uncertainty. Without explicit
055   mechanisms to identify and assess the assumptions that underlie uncertainty in partially observable
056   environments, larger models still struggle to weigh competing hypotheses about the environment,
057   which leads to misaligned priorities and degraded planning quality.
058
      To address these limitations, we leverage two key empirical observations: First, when asked to
059
      evaluate or select actions, LLM planners internally generate implicit assumptions about uncertain
060
      aspects of the environment in their zero-shot Chain-of-Thought reasoning traces. For example, they
061   tend to hypothesize the presence of an unseen object or infer the likely action of a collaborator. These
062   assumptions can in turn be used to identify what information is missing. Second, we found that such
063   assumptions are invoked locally and referenced implicitly, without being explicitly aggregated for
064   a global decision. This unstructured handling prevents the planner from systematically reconciling
065   multiple assumptions, which hampers its ability to detect logical conflicts, compare expected gains,
066   weigh alternative actions consistently, and ultimately achieve higher planning accuracy.
067   We therefore propose Planner-Composer-Evaluator (PCE) to extract and aggregate these internally
068   generated assumptions into a single structured representation in the form of a decision tree, enabling
069   them to be jointly evaluated for more reliable and uncertainty-aware action selection. In this tree,
070   each internal node specifies an assumption about the environment, and each leaf node specifies the
071   action considered appropriate under the accumulated assumptions along the path. Thus, each root-
072   to-leaf path represents a particular combination of assumptions culminating in an action trajectory.
073   We then introduce an evaluator that scores each path in terms of its likelihood, gain, and cost, guiding
074   rational action selection without heavy communication. This design transforms the latent knowledge
075
      already produced by LLM reasoning into a principled mechanism for uncertainty-aware planning.
076   Unlike prior multi-agent planners (Seo et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Zu et al., 2025) that primarily
077   optimize coordination through iterative communication or joint plan negotiation, PCE operates at a
078   different level of the decision problem. Instead of optimizing over action sequences or communica-
079   tion strategies, PCE explicitly treats environmental assumptions as first-class decision variables and
080   reasons over them before action execution. This shifts the planning paradigm from communication-
081
      centric coordination to structured reasoning over uncertainty embedded in the agent’s own belief
      state.
082
083   Experiments on two challenging multi-agent benchmarks, C-WAH (Zhang et al., 2024b) and TDW-
084   MAT (Zhang et al., 2024b), show that PCE consistently outperforms communication-centric base-
085   lines in success rate, task efficiency, and token usage. Ablation studies reveal that increasing LLM
086   capacity or its reasoning depth without explicit uncertainty handling yields only limited perfor-
087
      mance improvements relative to its computational cost. User studies further demonstrate improved
      reliability in human-agent collaboration. Because the proposed framework operates on generic rea-
088
      soning traces rather than model-specific internals, it can be readily applied to a wide range of LLM
089
      backbones and multi-agent domains. We demonstrate this generality by running PCE on diverse
090   backbones, including GPT-4o mini, GPT-OSS:20B, and Gemma3:4B, and observe consistent im-
091   provements across all of them. Together, these results shift the focus from communication-heavy
092   mitigation to principled exploitation of the assumptions already present in LLM reasoning, opening
093   a new direction for uncertainty-aware planning in embodied systems.

096   2    R ELATED W ORK
097
098   Embodied Multi-Agent Cooperation. Embodied agents aim to achieve long-term goals in com-
099   plex environments populated with diverse objects (Song et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). Recent work
100   has leveraged LLMs as planners to decompose high-level goals into executable action sequences for
101   navigation and manipulation tasks (Zhou et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Wang
102   et al., 2023a;c) and to enhance planning effectiveness through zero-shot and few-shot reasoning as
103   well as dynamic replanning (Huang et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023). Beyond single-agent settings,
104
      cooperative planning in multi-agent environments has been studied to address tasks that exceed the
      capacity of an individual agent (Zhang et al., 2024c; Guo et al., 2024).
105
106   To study more realistic embodied multi-agent cooperation, researchers focus on environments where
107   distributed agents must make decisions based on their own limited observations (Zhang et al., 2024b;
      Seo et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Zu et al., 2025). In these settings, agents cannot acquire information


                                                          2
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




108
      beyond their observation range in real time. They must therefore either move physically to gather
109   information or share it through communication with collaborators to reduce travel time (Oliehoek &
110   Spaan, 2012; Spaan et al., 2006; Foerster et al., 2016).
111
112
      Prior work has mitigated uncertainty through intensive communication. For example, ProA-
      gent (Zhang et al., 2024a) explicitly reasons about collaborators’ actions and verifies its reasoning
113
      through dialogue; CoELA (Zhang et al., 2024b) and REVECA (Seo et al., 2025) exchange state and
114
      plan information to validate and adjust strategies; RoCo (Mandi et al., 2024) and CaPo (Liu et al.,
115   2025) introduce iterative inter-agent debates; and CoTS (Zu et al., 2025) exploits communication-
116   based feedback to refine joint plans. While these approaches improve coordination, they incur high
117   token and time costs and can disrupt workflows when humans are in the loop.
118
      More recently, LLaMAR (Nayak et al., 2024) has introduced a long-horizon LLM-based planning
119
      framework that operates in partially observable environments without relying on heavy communi-
120
      cation, yet its formulation addresses coordination in a centralized multi-agent setting.
121
122   Our work takes a different path: instead of relying on heavy communication, we extract and ag-
123   gregate the implicit assumptions in LLM reasoning into a decision tree framework that jointly eval-
124
      uates them for rational action selection. This enables agents to adaptively balance physical and
      communicative actions, reducing communication overhead while preserving or improving planning
125
      performance.
126
127
128   Scaling LLM for Embodied Agents. Recent advances in LLMs have enabled natural language
129
      reasoning and high-level decision making for embodied agents. Scaling model capacity improves
      performance (Kaplan et al., 2020) but requires substantial training time and data resources. To
130
      complement these costs, reinforcement learning fine-tuning has been proposed at the training
131
      stage (Shao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025), while reasoning-augmentation meth-
132   ods such as Chain-of-Thought (Wei et al., 2022), Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023), and Self-
133   Consistency (Wang et al., 2023b) have been introduced at inference time (Snell et al., 2025).
134
135
      However, merely increasing model capacity or reasoning depth does not address the fundamental
      reliance on knowledge stored within model parameters (Mirzadeh et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023;
136
      Yin et al., 2024). This limitation is evident in tasks requiring external knowledge (Lewis et al.,
137
      2020), where LLMs produce uncertainty-laden outputs with degraded quality (Li et al., 2025), in
138   partially observable, decentralized environments where agents’ internal memory quickly becomes
139   outdated, amplifying uncertainty and restricting planning performance (Spaan et al., 2006; Amato
140   et al., 2016; 2015).
141
      To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has systematically examined whether uncertainty in
142
      embodied planning can be resolved simply by scaling LLMs. Our experiments show that beyond
143
      the gains from increasing model capacity or reasoning depth, PCE delivers robust improvements
144   across all tested backbones, indicating that its benefits are additive to and distinct from those of
145   scaling.
146
147
      Tree-Structured Reasoning and Search. Tree-based frameworks have been widely adopted to
148
      enhance decision quality, but PCE is conceptually distinct from existing approaches such as Tree of
149
      Thoughts (ToT) (Yao et al., 2023) and CoTS (Zu et al., 2025) in both what the tree represents and
150   how communication is treated. ToT constructs a tree over reasoning steps to enhance logical co-
151   herence, but it operates primarily within the internal reasoning space of a single agent. It implicitly
152   assumes a fully observable environment and treats tree nodes as cognitive steps rather than proba-
153   bilistic environmental states, limiting its applicability in dynamic, partially observable multi-agent
154   scenarios. In multi-agent settings, CoTS performs tree search over the joint-reasoning-and-action
155   space and uses iterative communication to explore and prune candidate plans. This communication-
156   centric formulation makes dialogue a prerequisite for search, resulting in increased latency and
157   significant token consumption, especially under imperfect observability.
158   In contrast, PCE structures the decision tree based on uncertain assumptions regarding the envi-
159   ronment rather than just reasoning steps. Crucially, PCE treats communication not as the search
160   mechanism itself, but as an atomic action within the search space to be evaluated against physical
161   actions. This allows the agent to select communication only when it yields higher expected utility
      than acting alone.

185

186

187   agents execute actions (a1 , . . . , an ) with transition probability P (s′ | s, a1 , . . . , an ).

                                                                  i      i

      ronmental observations with communication-based observations. Observation histories evolve as
      hit = (hit−1 , oit ).

192   The action space Ai = Aiphy ∪ Aicom contains both physical and communication actions. A lo-
193   cal policy π i maps the current history to an action, ait = π i (hit ). Communication actions transmit

249   4.2     P LANNER : R EASONING FOR ACTION S ELECTION
250
251
      The planner forms the first stage of our Planning Module. It receives as context input the common
      goal G, current progress, message logs, recent actions from memory, and available action list. Us-
252
      ing this, it reasons about why each available action could contribute to achieving G and produces
253
      an initial action choice accordingly. For example, if the agent has reached the kitchen and ob-
254   served a cabinet but not yet interacted with it, the Planner may propose an action such as ‘[gocheck]
255   <kitchencabinet> (78)’. Leveraging the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, the Planner outputs not
256   only a candidate action but also the associated reasoning trace.
257
      Empirically, each candidate action in this trace tends to be grounded in a single partial assump-
258
      tion about the uncertain environment. For example, as shown in Figure 2-(a), the available ac-
259
      tion ‘[gocheck] <bathroomcabinet> (25)’ is grounded solely in the assumption that ‘you might
260   find something useful here,’ while other assumptions relevant to alternative actions remain implicit.
261   These traces therefore reveal isolated assumption-action links but do not show how different as-
262   sumptions relate to each other, limiting the Planner’s ability to rank actions under uncertainty.
263
264   4.3     C OMPOSER : F ROM R EASONING TRACE TO S CENARIO T REE
265
266   The Composer organizes an explicit decision tree. It begins by semantically interpreting the context
267   input and the Planner’s reasoning trace to explicitly identify key uncertainties (assumptions). Inter-
268   nal nodes represent these assumptions with True/False splits whose branch conditions are inherited
269   along the path. A root-to-leaf path therefore defines a scenario, which is a combination of assump-
      tions culminating in a leaf node. Specifically, each leaf node is assigned an action aimed at handling


                                                                               5
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




270
      the uncertainty of the given path—either a physical action for direct interaction or a communication
271   action for sharing information or instructing the collaborator.
272
273   Instead of enumerating all assumption-action relationships, the Composer expands the tree top-
274
      down. At each node, a local ranking policy selects the next assumption to branch on, prioritizing
      those that most reduce uncertainty and most strongly influence subsequent action choice. Rather
275
      than computing true probabilities, which would amount to solving an intractable POMDP, we ap-
276
      proximate these criteria using LLMs’ commonsense reasoning. Only assumptions not yet assigned
277   on the path and consistent with current premises are considered, preventing incompatible scenarios
278   from being generated.
279
280
      When no suitable assumptions remain, the Composer proposes new atomic assumptions grounded
      in entities already present in the context (G, message logs, etc.). These candidates are ranked by
281
      the same policy and, if selected, inserted as new branches. Expansion is limited at depth D or stops
282
      early when further splits would not materially affect action choice; the resulting node becomes a leaf
283   and is assigned the most appropriate action. Multiple leaves may map to the same action, reflecting
284   its suitability across scenarios, while some initial actions may be dropped if never optimal.
285
286
      Figure 2-(b) illustrates this process. Given the goal of finding food items, a root assumption ‘the
      living room contains them’ leads the True branch toward a ‘[goexplore] <livingroom> (198)’. On
287
      the False branch, the premise changes, and the Composer generates a new assumption that the col-
288
      laborator (Bob) may know the cupcake’s location, shifting the plan from physical exploration to an
289   information gathering action such as [send message].

      4.4   E VALUATOR : S CENARIO LIKELIHOOD , CONDITIONAL GAIN , AND EXECUTION COST
292
293   The Evaluator takes the context input and the decision tree. It then scores each leaf to guide action
294   choice under uncertainty. For every root-to-leaf path, it estimates 1) how likely the scenario S is, 2)
295   how much the action would advance the goal if the scenario holds, and 3) how costly the action is to
296   attempt. All scores are normalized to [0, 1] for comparability.
297
298   Scenario likelihood (L). L(S) is the estimated probability that the premise of the leaf scenario is
299   true, assessed by an LLM against the agent’s observation and message history. For instance, if the
300
      collaborator was last seen in the kitchen, a scenario stating that the collaborator is still there receives
      a higher score than one claiming that the kitchen is empty.
301
302
      Conditional gain (G). G(a) measures how much executing action a would advance the goal given
303
      that the scenario is true. This value is also estimated by an LLM.
304
305   Exploiting L and G, we define the expected gain as
306                                           E[gain] = L(S) · G(a),
307
308
      where G(a) = 0 when the scenario is false.
309
      Execution cost (C). C(a) quantifies the immediate resources required to attempt a, regardless
310
      of its success. We decompose the cost into movement and communication terms using indicator
311
      functions:
312                         C(a) = α d(a) 1{move(a)} + β ℓ(a) 1{comm(a)},
313
      where d(a) is the estimated traversing distance, ℓ(a) is the estimated message length, α, β > 0
314
      are scaling constants that balance the cost of movement and communication, and 1{·} is the in-
315
      dicator. This design expresses the mutually exclusive nature of movement and communication:
316   1{move(a)} + 1{comm(a)} = 1.
317
318   Final score (U ). For each leaf action, the Evaluator computes a final score:
319
320
                                          U (S, a) = E[gain] − λ C(a),
321   where λ > 0 controlling cost sensitivity. This score integrates scenario probability, goal-directed
322   effectiveness, and execution expense. Ranking leaves by U (S, a) yields a rational action choice
323   under uncertainty, as shown in Figure 2-(c). The empirical setting of α, β, and λ is discussed in the
      experiments.

332   5     E XPERIMENT
333
334   Benchmarks. Under the constraint of partial observability in decentralized control, we conduct
335   experiments on two multi-objective household tasks composed of multiple rooms: C-WAH (Zhang
336   et al., 2024b) and TDW-MAT (Zhang et al., 2024b). C-WAH consists of 10 episodes, where agents
337   are required to accomplish 3–5 sub-goals in each episode, with the horizon H set to 250 steps.
338   TDW-MAT consists of 24 episodes, where agents are tasked with achieving the goal of transport-
339   ing 10 target objects, with the horizon H set to 3000 steps. Full environment details appear in
340   Appendix A.1.
341
342   Metrics. In C-WAH, we measure Total Steps to evaluate how quickly agents achieve the goal. In
343   TDW-MAT, we measure the proportion of target objects transported by the agents out of the total
344
      goal objects, grouped as Food, Stuff, and their average Total.
345   We also evaluate system efficiency using Usages and Comm. Usages denotes the total token con-
346   sumption generated by the entire system. This explicitly includes not only communication tokens
347   but also all internal tokens generated by the LLM modules within the framework, serving as a proxy
348   for total computational cost. Comm measures the number of communication actions. Unlike other
349   metrics, Comm does not have an intrinsic “better is lower” or “better is higher” interpretation. Com-
350
      munication actions are not direct goal completions but context-dependent interventions: in some
      cases, they slow progress through unnecessary exchanges, while in others, they avert false plans and
351
      improve coordination. We therefore treat Comm as a descriptive measure reported for diagnostic
352
      analysis rather than as a success metric, while primary comparisons focus on task performance and
353   token usage.
354
355
      Baselines. We compared PCE with four representative LLM-based cooperative agent frame-
356   works: CoELA (Zhang et al., 2024b), REVECA (Seo et al., 2025), CaPo (Liu et al., 2025), and
357   CoTS (Zu et al., 2025). CoELA first demonstrated LLM-driven cooperation for embodied agents.
358   REVECA introduced memory management, relative proximity-based planning, and plan valida-
359   tion. CaPo designed plan optimization through iterative debates, and CoTS integrated multi-plan
360   exploration with Monte Carlo Tree Search. All baselines are run under identical environmen-
361   tal and communication settings. For our PCE framework, we use the default hyperparameters
362   D = 3, α = 1, β = 1, λ = 1, Kaction = 10, Kmessage = 3. Further details of the baselines
363   can be found in Appendix A.2.
364
365   LLMs. To test robustness across model backbones, each framework is run on three di-

415   time on message exchanges and more time on physical actions. This behavior effectively suppresses
416   unnecessary communication-driven planning cycles, thereby reducing LLM inference cost. More-
417   over, although PCE’s three-module LLM architecture incurs higher per-step inference cost compared

426   limited ability to proactively identify highly uncertain factors and query them. CaPo and CoTS have
427   the advantage of generating multi-step plans, but in environments with high uncertainty, these plans
428
      often become invalid, leading to repeated replanning.
429   By contrast, PCE yields stable improvements across all backbones and environments by separating
430   assumption extraction, scenario structuring, and evaluation. This enables the agent to detect when
431   information exchange is genuinely useful, select between physical and communication actions in a
      principled way, and maintain high performance under decentralized partial observability.

456
457
      LLM Scaling. We first examine whether performance gains come from our structural design rather
      than simply scaling LLM capacity or reasoning depth. To this end, we compare PCE with a Planner
458
      only variant that removes the Composer and Evaluator, eliminating explicit uncertainty handling.
459
      As shown in Figure 3, even when the backbone size is increased from Gemma3:4B→12B→27B or
460   when GPT-OSS:20B’s reasoning depth is raised from Low→Medium→High, Planner only shows
461   only modest improvements, while PCE consistently achieves faster goal completion. This indicates
462   that fragmented, implicitly handled assumptions persist under mere scaling, leading to inefficient
463   exploration and occasional planning errors. In contrast, PCE’s Composer–Evaluator explicitly or-
464   ganizes and scores these assumptions, demonstrating that our structured uncertainty handling syner-
465   gizes with scaling to provide a stable performance advantage regardless of the underlying backbone.
466
467   Component Analysis. We also ablate individual components of the PCE pipeline using GPT-4o
468   mini. As reported in Table 3, removing any module (w/o Planner, w/o Composer, w/o Evaluator)
469   reduces performance. Without the Planner, scenario trees are built directly from context. This in-
470
      creases the difficulty of scenario exploration and often results in incoherent or redundant branches.
      Without the Composer, the agent relies solely on the Planner’s reasoning trace for evaluation, which
471
      prevents it from accounting for conflicting or complementary relationships among multiple assump-
472
      tions and results in incomplete exploration of alternative scenarios. Without the Evaluator, actions
473   are selected without quantitative likelihood–gain–cost assessment, which degrades decision quality.
474   These results confirm that each module contributes essentially to uncertainty-aware planning.
475
476
      We performed additional analyses to stress-test the structural foundations of PCE and rigorously
      evaluate its reliability and scalability.
477
478   First, we constructed physical-only and communication-only variants of PCE and conducted com-
479   parative experiments, alongside separate comparisons between PCE and reasoning-centric baselines
480   (Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thoughts, Self-Consistency). These analyses confirm that PCE’s high
481   performance does not stem merely from more sophisticated reasoning traces, but from the explicit
482
      structuring and evaluation of uncertainty (Appendix A.5).
483   Second, we assessed the robustness of the framework through a series of quantitative and qualitative
484   evaluations: (1) scalability tests with increasing number of agents to verify cooperative efficiency
485   (Appendix A.9); (2) reliability assessments of the Composer and Evaluator based on human–expert
      correlation studies (Appendix A.10, A.11); (3) hyperparameter sensitivity analyses to examine con-

      5.3   U SER S TUDY R ESULTS
494
      In real human collaboration, communication is a double-edged sword: excessive messaging dis-
495   rupts workflow, while the absence of necessary exchanges makes intentions opaque and degrades
496   joint performance. We hypothesized that PCE’s ability to structure and evaluate assumptions would
497   allow it to trigger communication only when genuinely useful, producing behavior that humans
498   perceive as aligned, efficient, and trustworthy. To test this, we ran a user study in the C-WAH envi-
499   ronment comparing (1) PCE, (2) w/o Com (communication actions removed), and (3) Com always
500   (communication forced before each physical action). Twelve participants (mean age 26.8; 8 male, 4
501   female) received the same observations and action choices as the agent.
502   After completing each method, participants answered four questions on a 7-point Likert scale (1:
503   strongly disagree, 7: strongly agree): 1) “Did the agent perform actions appropriate to your inten-
504   tions?” (Appropriateness), 2) “Was the agent helpful in collaboration?” (Usefulness), 3) “Did the
505   agent’s performance contribute to achieving the goal efficiently?” (Efficiency), and “4) Did you feel
506   a sense of trust with the agent?” (Trust). They then joined brief qualitative interviews.
507   As shown in Figure 4, PCE scored highest across all questions, confirming our hypothesis: by
508   reasoning over uncertainties and initiating communication selectively, PCE achieves a balance of
509   clarity and efficiency that users recognize as more cooperative and reliable. Interview feedback
510   noted that Com always disrupted workflows, while w/o Com made the agent’s intentions unclear and
511   harder to trust. Detailed results are in Appendix A.6.

      6     C ONCLUSION
514
515
      This paper presented PCE, a modular Planner-Composer-Evaluator framework that extracts and
516   structures implicit assumptions embedded in LLM reasoning traces, enabling embodied agents to
517   plan under partial observability with minimal communication. Across two multi-objective bench-
518   marks (C-WAH and TDW-MAT) and three diverse LLM backbones, PCE consistently outperformed
519   communication-centric baselines in success rate and task efficiency while showing comparable to-
520   ken usage. Ablation studies confirmed that each module is indispensable and that explicit uncer-
521   tainty handling unlocks performance gains beyond what model scaling alone provides. A user study
522   further showed that PCE produces communication patterns that humans perceive as efficient and
523   trustworthy.
524   While our experiments focus on simulated multi-room household tasks, the proposed mechanism is
525   not tied to a specific domain. Future work will explore extending PCE to more complex and dynamic
526   environments, larger and more diverse agent teams, and adaptive discovery of new assumptions
527   in real time. These directions aim to test the framework’s scalability and generality under richer
528   uncertainty, advancing the broader goal of principled, decentralized cooperation with embodied
529   agents.

      A.2   BASELINES
811
812   CoELA (Zhang et al., 2024b) implements a modular LLM-driven multi-agent architecture: each
813   agent has perception, memory, planning, communication, and execution modules. Agents exchange
814   linguistic updates and reason over what to do next. It is decentralized and flexible, and with strong
815   LLMs can achieve good multi-agent coordination in embodied tasks.
816   REVECA (Seo et al., 2025) proposes an LLM-based cooperative agent architecture that leverages
817   information relevance and relative proximity for adaptive planning, and employs trajectory-based
818   validation to prevent false planning caused by collaborators’ actions, yielding more efficient memory
819   use and stronger coordination under partial observability.
820
      CaPo (Liu et al., 2025) introduces a collaborative meta-plan generation phase, where agents jointly
821
      construct a high-level task decomposition and allocate subtasks before execution. During the task,
822   agents engage in progress-adaptive re-planning, dynamically updating the meta-plan when new in-
823   formation or environmental changes are observed. This two-stage process of structured plan con-
824   struction followed by adaptive revision enhances coordination, reduces redundancy, and improves
825   task efficiency in embodied multi-agent settings.
826
      CoTS (Zu et al., 2025) introduces a collaborative tree search framework for multi-agent planning,
827
      inspired by Monte Carlo Tree Search. Instead of following a single plan path, agents explore mul-
828   tiple candidate branches, evaluate them using LLM-based reasoning or heuristic scoring, and then
829   converge on a coherent long-term strategy. A dedicated plan evaluation module monitors execution
830   progress and selectively triggers plan updates when necessary, striking a balance between adaptabil-
831   ity and stability. This design provides greater foresight, improves resilience to planning errors, and
832   reduces unnecessary disruptions during collaboration.

      When only an available action list is provided, LLM agents often struggle to account for efficient
897
      movement paths or division of labor that enhances cooperative efficiency during planning. There-

1140   A.9    S CALABILITY A NALYSIS : I MPACT OF N UMBER OF AGENTS
1141
1142   A fundamental challenge in multi-agent planning is the potential for combinatorial explosion as
1143   the number of agents (N ) increases. In this section, we analyze the scalability of PCE from both
1144   structural and empirical perspectives, demonstrating how the framework manages complexity in
1145   teams larger than two agents.
1146
1147   Structural Efficiency via Semantic Aggregation. Extracting uncertainty directly from the full
1148   joint state of all agents would lead to combinatorial complexity in decision tree construction. In
1149
       contrast, our Composer is designed to extract uncertainty solely from the Planner’s reasoning trace,
       observations, and memory. Because the Planner inherently filters out irrelevant details to focus on
1150
       salient uncertainties, the Composer’s search space scales with the complexity of the uncertainty
1151
       itself, rather than linearly with the number of agents.
1152

1217   A.11     R ELIABILITY A NALYSIS OF THE C OMPOSER M ODULE
1218
1219
       The Composer takes the Planner’s free-form reasoning trace as input, restructures it into explicit
       assumptions, and uses them to construct a decision tree. Therefore, its reliability is critical: if the
1220
       Composer extracts incorrect information or generates hallucinations, the subsequent decision tree
1221
       becomes invalid. To rigorously assess this reliability, we conducted two complementary analyses:
1222   1) a quantitative stress test at the assumption level, and 2) an expert evaluation at the decision-tree
1223   level. The first experiment focuses on how effectively the Composer identifies critical assumptions
1224   even when the Planner’s reasoning trace is vague. The second experiment evaluates whether these
1225   assumptions are organized into a coherent decision-tree structure.
1226
1227   A.11.1    Q UANTITATIVE S TRESS T EST AT THE A SSUMPTION L EVEL
1228
1229   To evaluate whether the Composer reliably identifies critical assumptions, including in cases where
1230   the Planner’s reasoning trace is vague or incomplete, we designed a quantitative stress test at the
1231
       level of individual assumption nodes. Since the correctness of extracted or generated assumptions is
       inherently semantic and context-dependent, it cannot be reliably validated by rule-based metrics or
1232
       environment rewards alone. Human experts are therefore required to serve as the only trustworthy
1233
       reference for judging whether an assumption genuinely reflects the Planner’s intent or constitutes a
1234   reasonable hypothesis in ambiguous contexts. The primary outcome metric for this experiment is
1235   the proportion of assumptions judged valid by human experts, which we refer to as the Composer’s
1236   hit rate.
1237
1238   Experimental Setup The experiment utilized execution logs collected from 10 C-WAH tasks. For
1239   each log, we modified the Composer’s prompt so that every assumption node explicitly self-labels
1240   as one of the following types:
1241
              • Extracted: Assumptions directly grounded in the Planner’s reasoning trace.

1247   assumptions that are inferable but embedded in ambiguous or cluttered reasoning. Hard cases
1248   correspond to vague traces where the critical assumption is not directly mentioned, requiring implicit
