009                                                     A BSTRACT
010
011                When are multi-agent LLM systems merely a collection of individual agents
012                versus an integrated collective with higher-order structure? We introduce an
013                information-theoretic framework to test—in a purely data-driven way—whether
014                multi-agent systems show signs of higher-order structure. This information de-
015                composition lets us measure whether dynamical emergence is present in multi-
016                agent LLM systems, localize it, and distinguish spurious temporal coupling from
017
                   performance-relevant cross-agent synergy. We implement both a practical cri-
                   terion and an emergence capacity criterion operationalized as partial informa-
018
                   tion decomposition of time-delayed mutual information (TDMI). We apply our
019
                   framework to experiments using a simple guessing game without direct agent
020                communication and only minimal group-level feedback with three randomized
021                interventions. Groups in the control condition exhibit strong temporal synergy
022                but only little coordinated alignment across agents. Assigning a persona to each
023                agent introduces stable identity-linked differentiation. Combining personas with
024                an instruction to “think about what other agents might do” shows identity-linked
025                differentiation and goal-directed complementarity across agents. Taken together,
026                our framework establishes that multi-agent LLM systems can be steered with
027                prompt design from mere aggregates to higher-order collectives. Our results
028
                   are robust across emergence measures and entropy estimators, and not explained
                   by coordination-free baselines or temporal dynamics alone. Without attributing
029
                   human-like cognition to the agents, the patterns of interaction we observe mirror
030
                   well-established principles of collective intelligence in human groups: effective
031                performance requires both alignment on shared objectives and complementary
032                contributions across members.

035   1       I NTRODUCTION
036
037   Recent advances in generative AI (specifically LLMs) have led to tremendous advances in multi-
038   agent systems (Qu et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024a; Subramaniam
039   et al., 2025). Multi-agent systems often show impressive performance increases over single-agent
040   solutions (Wu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b; Tao et al., 2024). A key argument
041   behind such performance gains are claims to “greater-than-the-sum-of-its-parts” effects from differ-
042
      entiated agents (Chen et al., 2023, p.1). Connecting multiple differentiated agents has the potential
      benefit to leverage unique contributions that would not be available if the task was assigned to only
043
      a single agent (Fazelpour & De-Arteaga, 2022; Tollefsen et al., 2013; Luppi et al., 2024; Lix et al.,
044
      2022). Conceptually, any group gains depend on emergence and synergy (Theiner, 2018; Riedl
045   et al., 2021; Page, 2008; Hayek, 1945).1 Despite impressive performance of many multi-agent sys-
046   tems we do not yet have a principled understanding when and how such synergy emerges, what role
047   agent differentiation plays, and how to steer it systematically. This all points to a crucial need for
048   deeper understanding of collective intelligence in multi-agent systems, specifically whether multi-
049   agent LLMs exhibit any synergy or complementarity at all—the necessary prerequisite for absolute
050   team-over-solo gains.
051   In this paper, we address the question when is a population of LLM agents a mere collection vs. a
052   higher-order collective? We follow a purely data-driven approach to assess whether multi-agent

      systems show signs of emergence in the form of higher-order synergy characterized by structural
055   coupling and joint information about future states and task outcomes. Intuitively, synergy refers
056   to information about a target that a collection of variables provide only jointly but not individually
057   (Rosas et al., 2020; Humphreys, 1997).2 We develop a principled framework (Figure 1a) building
058   on new insights in information theory (Rosas et al., 2020; Mediano et al., 2022). Our goal is to
059   develop methods to measure emergent role specialization, determine whether multi-agent systems
060   show such signs of emergent synergy, explore whether synergy enables increased performance, and
061   explore whether emergent behavior can be systematically steered with prompting.
062   We apply our framework to study multi-agent systems of gpt-4.1 and Llama-3.1-8B agents solving
063   a simple group guessing task (Figure 1b) with three treatment interventions: a control condition,
064   a condition that assigns personas to each agent, and one that uses personas with an instruction to
065   “think about what other agents might do” (a theory of mind (ToM) prompt). The paper is organized
066   into addressing three concrete research questions:
067
068
          RQ1: Do multi-agent LLM systems possess the capacity for emergence?
069       RQ2: What functional advantages—such as synergistic coordination and higher performance—
070            arise when multi-agent systems exhibit emergence?
071       RQ3: Can we design prompts, roles, or reasoning structures that steer the internal coordination
072            of multi-agent to encourage positive, goal-directed synergy?
073
074
      Our findings provide evidence for multi-agent LLM system capacity for emergence and that it under-
      pins performance. Through a variety of complementary analyses and robustness tests, we show that
075
      coordination style across interventions is very different. While emergence is present in all, only the
076
      ToM-prompt condition leads to groups with identity-linked differentiation and goal-directed com-
077   plementarity across agents: they operate as an integrated, goal-directed unit. This is consistent with
078   research on human groups showing that performance gains are not automatic (e.g., Stasser & Titus,
079   1985) and that complementarity needs to be balanced with integration and goal alignment (Theiner,
080   2018; DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus, 2010; Riedl et al., 2021).
081
      This paper helps us understand when and how multi-agent systems exhibit higher-order properties,
082
      how to control them, and their internal coordination. This can inform multi-agent system design by
083   showing how to combine agents effectively. We make four contributions:
084
085          1. Novel framework to quantify emergent properties in multi-agent systems based on infor-
086             mation decomposition including conditional/residual variants and outcome-relevant partial
087             information decomposition.
088          2. Principled diagnostic approaches to localize where synergy resides and distinguish it
089             from alternative explanations (such as heterogeneous learning rates). Specifically, we de-
090             velop two surrogate null distribution tests (row-shuffle to probe identity-locked structure;
091             column-shuffle for dynamic alignment). This design lets us tease apart “good” synergy
092             aligned with task goals from spurious or misaligned synergy.
093          3. Demonstrate how to steer emergence with specific prompts. Prompt-level manipula-
094             tions causally change higher-order dependencies and reliably induces distinct coordina-
095             tion regimes, shifting collectives from spurious and misdirected synergy to stable & goal-
096             aligned complementarity driven by differentiated identities.
097          4. Internal coordination is measurable and controllable with interventions. Groups differ in
098             variance, stability, and adaptability—properties relevant to reliability and deployment.

137   Analytical Framework. How would we know if a multi-agent system shows emergent properties
138   that could suggest that the sum is more than its parts? We develop a principled test based on a recent
139   formal theory of dynamic emergence based on information decomposition (Rosas et al., 2020; Medi-
140
      ano et al., 2022; Bedau & Humphreys, 2008). Our framework connects emergence with information
      about a system’s temporal evolution—future states of the whole—with information that cannot be
141
      traced to the current state of its parts. This framework is based on partial information decomposition
142
      (PID; Williams & Beer, 2010) and time-delayed mutual information (TDMI; Luppi et al., 2022),
143   is data-driven, quantifiable, and amenable to empirical testing with falsifiable conjectures against
144   specific null hypotheses. Combining this with permutation tests, focused either on breaking connec-
145   tions between agents or connections across time allow us not only to quantity emergent properties
146   but also to localize them in the system. This is crucial for multi-agent systems because we care about
147   distinguishing synergy among specialized, differentiated agents from mere dynamic alignment.
148
      We implement three tests. The first, termed emergence capacity, captures ability of the multi-agent
149   system to host any synergy. It measures the predictive synergy from two agents’ current states to
150   their joint future state. For each pair of agents (i, j) and time t, let the sources be Xi,t and Xj,t ,
151   and define the bivariate target as the next-step joint state Tij,t+ℓ ≡ (Xi,t+ℓ , Xj,t+ℓ ). From the
152   joint contingency table over (Xi,t , Xj,t , Tij,t+ℓ ) we compute a two-source PID of the predictive
153   information

224   Entropy Estimation. Small-sample entropy estimation is often challenging because the true dis-
225   tribution is only partially observed and many outcome categories receive few or even zero counts
226
      (“empty bins”). Such finite-sample estimation of information measures is biased upward and can
      yield spurious positive findings. Bias grows with more bins, dimensionality (e.g., using triplets
227
      instead of pairs), and small N (few timesteps). The empirical plug-in estimator replace true proba-
228
      bilities with sample frequencies and unseen (or under-sampled) outcomes often are assigned zero or
229   very low probabilities (Hausser & Strimmer, 2009). To account for this issue, we follow best prac-
230   tices and take several steps. First, we compute the emergence capacity and the coalition tests as order
231   k = 2 instead of the entire system of n agents. Second, we apply quantile binning (with two bins) to
232   reduce the dimensionality of our data (sensitivity analyses with three bins reported in A.8). Third,
233   we use bias-corrected entropy estimator with Jeffreys’ prior, which smooths estimates and avoids
234   empty bins by adding α = 12 pseudo-counts in the Dirichlet estimator (Jeffreys, 1946). This reduces
235   systematic inflation and cures zero-count pathologies. Finally, as a robustness check, we also report
236   results using the Miller–Madow bias-corrected estimator (Miller, 1955), and the MMI redundancy
237
      measure (MMI typically overestimates redundancy, making synergy estimates more conservative;
      Mediano et al., 2025).

285   To see if LLMs can solve this task and how sensitive results are to different group sizes and temper-

289   sizes × 11 temperature settings × 50 groups = 7,150 experiments; Figure 1c). Multi-agent systems
290   of gpt-4.1 agents can solve the task reliably but encounter substantial challenges. We fit a logistic
291   regression, predicting the probability of success from group size and temperature. We find the task is
292   significantly easier for smaller groups: each additional group member decreased the odds by roughly

      8% (OR = 0.92, p < 10−16 ). This replicates the same results as for human groups (Goldstone et al.,
      2024). Each unit increase in temperature increased the odds of success by approximately 50% (OR

      4.2   DYNAMICAL M ECHANISMS
355
356   How is this emergence dynamically maintained and where is it located within the groups? We find
357   time-trend demeaned bias-corrected I3 around 0 in the Plain (Wilcoxon p-value 0.974) and Persona
358   condition (p = 0.846) indicating groups that fail to escape chaotic, uncoordinated states. In contrast,
359   the ToM shows significant positive mutual information (p = 3.5×10−14 ), collapsing the group’s be-
360   havior into a predictable macro-state (Figure A2)). The number of groups with significantly positive
361   I3 is substantially higher in the ToM condition (Figure 3a). To formalize this transition dynamic,
362   we compute the Total Stability of the system (computed as I3 normalized by the entropy of the
363
      macro-signal) which serves as a proxy for the Lyapunov stability of the collective state (Haddad &
      Chellaboina, 2008). We find Total Stability indistinguishable form zero in the Plain (Wilcoxon p-
364
      value 0.976) and Persona (p-value = 0.858) indicating a “gaseous” state where groups fail to settle
365
      into a stable attractor. However, the ToM intervention induces a sharp increase in Total Stability
366   (p-value = 2.9 × 10−14 ). Theoretically, this suggests the ToM prompt acts as a control parameter,
367   steering the multi-agent system from a chaotic regime into a deep basin of attraction where collective
368   behavior stabilizes: turning the system from a collection of individuals into a collective group.
369
      Decomposition this stability reveals the specific mechanism of coordination. While the system ex-
370
      hibits strong emergence capacity and practical emergence, we find the stability in group behavior
371
      is supported by pairwise alignment rather than irreducible triplet complexity. Specifically, triadic
372   information gain (G3) is around 0 in Persona and ToM (Figure 3c). The Plain conditions shows
373   small positive G3 > 0 (Wilcoxon p-value = 0.026) but given the near-zero Total Stability, this likely
374   reflects transient stochastic correlations and oscillation rather than sustained coordination. In dy-
375   namical systems terms, the ToM prompt creates a deep basin of attraction where agents converge
376   on a shared schematic (or Schelling point), making the collective state robust but informationally
377   redundant. This mirrors “Mean Field” coupling in physics: because agents receive only global feed-
      back and cannot observe individual peers, they couple to the aggregate signal rather than forming


                                                                                                                  7
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




378
      distinct local bonds. In such an environment, attempting complex higher-order synergy would be
379   fragile; instead, the system converges on the efficient solution of dense pairwise alignment to the
380   Mean Field.
381
382
      Next, we explore whether agents evolve specialized roles & identities. Are there detectable between-
      agent differences in either the level of their contribution to the group guess or their temporal evolu-
383
      tion (i.e., learning rate)? Using the hierarchical model comparison test we find that agents in may
384
      groups have differentiated identities (Figure 3a; a Fisher test for joint p-value evidence is again
385   highly significant). There is substantially more differentiation among agents in the Persona con-
386   dition, and even more in the ToM condition. Inspecting the reasoning traces of agents, they often
387   contain references to the “personal experience” of their assigned persona such as “In my experience
388   (whether it’s corraling cattle or wrangling numbers), starting toward the middle gives the group the
389   best shot to zero in after the first feedback” (see Appendix for additional sample quotes). In Ap-
390   pendix A.10 we provide additional robustness tests ruling out heterogeneous learning rates as the
391   sole source of differentiation and provide additional evidence for stable agent identities that persist
392   over time. That is, in the Plain condition, differentiation results only from idiosyncratic noise and
393
      transient drift introduced by probabilistic LLM responses. With Persona, agents gain stable, iden-
      tity linked behavioral preferences that refine their differentiation. The addition of ToM sharpens
394
      role differentiation through feedback loops from mutual predictive modeling by conditioning on the
395
      public history (which functions as a common ground and coordination device; Lewis, 1969; Skyrms,
396   2010). This converts small persona-induced asymmetries into stable, self-reinforcing roles, creating
397   the basis for strategic complementarity (answering RQ2 and RQ3)

      4.3   F UNCTIONAL ROLE OF E MERGENCE IN G ROUP P ERFORMANCE
400
401
      What functional differences—if any—does emergence allow? We use regression analysis to ex-
402   plore the joint effect of synergy and redundancy (based on emergence capacity formulation) while
403   carefully controlling for endogeneity of variables measuring emergent properties and performance
404   and controlling for sample selection bias with stabilized inverse probability weighting (see Ap-
405   pendix A.11 for method details). On their own, higher levels of either synergy or redundancy do not
406   predict success. However, when both are present, performance improves significantly (significant
407   interaction with β = 0.24; p = 0.014). In marginal-effect terms, redundancy amplifies the benefit
408   of synergy on the log-odds scale by 27%; and vice versa synergy amplifies benefits of redundancy
409   by 27%. This pattern implies that systems benefit when redundant pathways create goal alignment,
410
      while synergistic interactions extract novel, non-overlapping information—together enabling higher
      overall performance. We complement the regression analysis with causal mediation analysis (Imai
411
      et al., 2010). While reaching only marginal significant levels the effect is consistent: the ToM treat-
412
      ment causally increases performance indirectly by increasing synergy (ACME = 0.034 [95%CI:
413   −0.000 − 0.07], p = 0.053). This aligns with the interpretation that performance benefits emerge
414   when systems achieve both redundancy (aligned toward a common goal) and synergistic integration
415   (differentiated, complementary roles)—a form of functional and collective complexity (Varley et al.,
416   2023; Luppi et al., 2024; Sterelny, 2007).

462   5                                            R ELATED W ORK
463
464   Shortly after the release of ChatGPT, the community began exploring multi-agent LLM systems,
465   where multiple LLM agents interact with each other. One early example is Park et al. (2023), who
466   simulated a small population of “individuals” in a The Sims-style environment with friends, houses,
467   and jobs, showing the emergence of rich social behaviors such as coordinating a Valentine’s Day
468   party. This was followed by multi-agent systems for complex tasks such as software development
469   (Chen et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2023), healthcare (Li et al., 2024a), and other
470   domains (Subramaniam et al., 2025; Ashery et al., 2025). Federated systems or “society of models”
471
      also gained attention (Juneja et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024b). These systems often achieve significant
      performance gains over single-agent baselines (Wu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b;
472
      Tao et al., 2024).
473
474   A recurring explanation for such gains invokes “greater-than-the-sum-of-its-parts” effects (Chen
475   et al., 2023, p.1), often attributed to division of labor among differentiated agents (Subramaniam
476   et al., 2025). Yet while this work is “inspired by human group dynamics” (Chen et al., 2023,
477
      p.1), it rarely evaluates whether the same principles that underlie effective (and ineffective) hu-
      man groups also emerge in multi-agent systems. Two gaps stand out. First, human groups often
478
      coordinate by developing role specialization (Goldstone et al., 2024). Multi-agent systems often in-
479
      tuitively induce role specialization (such as “programmer”, “tester”, and “CEO”; Qian et al., 2023)
480   yet have not systematically evaluated the impact on emergent shared cognition beyond win-rate
481   comparisons. Second, groups only outperform individuals if their members specialize in contribut-
482   ing different pieces of information to the group task (Theiner, 2013; DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus,
483   2010; Fazelpour & De-Arteaga, 2022; Nickerson & Zenger, 2004). Effective groups balance
484   complementarity—members contributing distinct information—with redundancy, the alignment on
485   shared goals (Theiner, 2013; DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus, 2010; Fazelpour & De-Arteaga, 2022;
      Luppi et al., 2024; Tollefsen et al., 2013). Too much of either undermines performance. Unlike win-


                                                                                                                                      9
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




486
      rate–centric evaluations—and tests of whether agents in multi-agent systems can sustain cooperation
487   per se Piedrahita et al. (2025); Piatti et al. (2024)—we propose falsification tests and null modeling,
488   thus extending multi-agent frameworks with formal theory. While these principles likely apply to
489   LLM-based collectives given the universality of the integration-segregation tradeoff (Tononi et al.,
490   1994), it remains unclear whether agents develop differentiated roles, whether such roles comple-
491   ment each other, how to steer it with prompts, and what role ToM capacity of models plays for
492   collaboration (Westby & Riedl, 2023; Kleiman-Weiner et al., 2025; Riedl & Weidmann, 2025).

      6   C ONCLUSION
495
496
      Collectives of LLM agents are emerging as a powerful new paradigm, capable of tackling tasks that
497
      exceed the reach of any single model. Yet raw capability alone may not determine their effectiveness.
498
      Effective multi-agent systems depend on acting as integrated, cohesive units as opposed to loose ag-
499   gregations of individual agents. In collective intelligence, this distinction often separates groups that
500   merely average their parts from those that achieve true synergy (Riedl et al., 2021). This paper asks
501   whether such coordination dynamics also matter for LLM collectives, what functional benefits they
502   have, and whether we can design prompts, roles, or reasoning structures that foster such integrated
503   yet synergistic behavior. The framework we developed connects human group cognition theory to
504   LLM multi-agent systems, offering a novel conceptual bridge. Methodologically, we demonstrate
505   how to operationalize group-level synergy in AI collectives using quantitative information-theoretic
506   measures. We also provide specific design principles for creating productive LLM collectives that
507
      are aligned on “shared goals,” which can inform multi-agent orchestration tools, cooperative AI, and
      mixed human–AI teamwork. This work extends ToM research beyond standard false-belief tests to
508
      realistic collaborative tasks (Shapira et al., 2024; 2023). Those insights will help us understand when
509
      multiple LLMs working together will be beneficial, why that is, and inform their design.
510
511   We note that evidence of higher-order synergy should not be interpreted as implying sophisticated
512   cognition or consciousness. As conceptualized here, synergy is a structural property of part-whole
513
      relationships within multi-agent interactions. Such higher-order structures are known to arise in sim-
      ple systems, including agents governed by reinforcement learning (Fulker et al., 2024) or via basic
514
      nonlinearities in contagion processes (Iacopini et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2025). Without attributing
515
      human-like cognition to the agents, the patterns of interaction we observe mirror well-established
516   principles of collective intelligence in human groups (Riedl et al., 2021; DeChurch & Mesmer-
517   Magnus, 2010): effective multi-agent performance requires both alignment on shared objectives and
518   complementary contributions across members.
519
520   Limitations and Future Work. This work focuses on developing a framework, characterizing
521   emergence, and localizing it. While we explore the degree of goal alignment via a performance-
522   related macro signal and performance effects of “early synergy”, the analysis linking synergy and
523   redundancy to performance is challenging because synergy and redundancy are often co-dependent
524   and emerge alongside performance. Future work should aim to more directly connect measures
525   of synergy with performance. Despite a significant number of robustness tests, estimating entropy
526   and establishing synergy is difficult. Furthermore, this work established results only using a sin-
527   gle task—albeit one that is uniquely suited to studying synergy given how closely it mirrors ideal
528
      case of complementary behavior in a minimalist setting. More work will be necessary to convinc-
      ingly establish when and how multi-agent LLM systems develop emergent synergy across tasks and
529
      measures. Given the small-data setting some of our information theoretic measures are limited to
530
      computing dynamic emergence on order k = 2 which is bound to miss synergy of a higher order.
531   Finally, this work points to the importance of ToM. LLMs capacity to act in a ToM-like manner ap-
532   pears crucial to achieve functional alignment in multi-agent systems. This should provide additional
533   motivation for research on ToM in LLMs.

       A.10    A RE I DENTITIES P ERSISTENT ?
973
974    If some agents learn faster than others, even without persistent “roles,” synergy can emerge simply
975    because differentiated trajectories create complementarity across time. As indeed the mixed model
976    test shows that 32% of groups have significant slopes provides strong evidence for the presence of
977    heterogeneous learning rates. That is, heterogeneous learning rates could be a confound explaining
978    some of our observed synergy effects. To rule out this alternative explanation we explore if agents
979    have persistent “identities.” We find the block-shuffle nulls show more significant G3 than the
980
       full row shuffle. This suggests that synergy depends on temporal continuity of identities (not just on
       different learning rates at each timepoint). Furthermore, the hierarchical model test shows significant
981
       variance even without varying slopes, indicating stable role-like differences beyond learning rates.
982
       Finally, our analyses removing time trends and the functional null-model also remove learning rate
983    slopes (Table A1). The functional null-model in particular does this in a non-linear way that is
984    directly tied to our task. In summary, we find both heterogeneous learning rates and stable identity-
985    linked differentiation. Heterogeneous learning rates alone cannot explain the observed emergent
986    synergy that persist across time (providing additional insights regarding RQ2).
987
       Together, the results suggest a nuanced picture of emergence in multi-agent systems. Persona
988
       and ToM produce agents with distinct, stable identities—moving agents on the differentiated-
989    undifferentiated axis in Figure 1a. This differentiation shapes the space in which other agents can
990    adapt, and specialization by one agent constrains the (useful) actions of the others (agents mutu-
991    ally constrain each other; cf. Tollefsen et al., 2013)—shifts on the interdependent-complementary
992    axis. When combined with ToM, mutual adaptation both strengthens agent differentiation (creating
993    the foundation for possible synergistic complementarity) while also increasing mutual alignment
994    on shared goals (the target signal)—shifts on the integration axis. Differentiation enables synergy,
995    but without redundancy and integration, synergy alone does not translate into higher collective per-
996    formance. Agents in the ToM condition develop specialized, complementary roles while achieving
997
       stronger shared goal alignment: they are simultaneously more differentiated yet also more integrated
       along highly overlapping task-relevant information which the capacity for synergy can now exploit
998
       in productive ways.

       A.11    A DDITIONAL D ETAILS FOR P ERFORMANCE A NALYSIS
1002
       What does synergy enable? In particular, does synergy affect performance? Connecting emergent
1003   properties of synergy and redundancy to performance is a challenging task for two main reasons.
1004   First, redundancy and synergy sit at opposite ends of the informational spectrum of correlations.
1005   Systems constrained by limited resources (such as energy or bandwidth) cannot maximize both
1006   simultaneously. The “informational budget” of correlations can be spent on shared overlap (redun-
1007   dancy) or on higher-order joint structures (synergy), but not both simultaneously (Williams & Beer,
1008   2010; Varley et al., 2023; Luppi et al., 2024). Second, emergent properties are dichromatic—they
1009   require time to evolve (Bedau, 2002)—making them endogenous with run-length of the simulation.
1010   Namely, simulations in which the multi-agent system performs worse and takes longer to solve the
1011
       task (or fails to solve the task altogether) provide them with more time to evolve sophisticated group
       coordination patterns; while successful runs terminate early, giving collectives less time to evolve
1012
       emergent properties. This complicates meaningful analyses as it can make it appear that higher syn-
1013
       ergy is correlated with failure. In technical terms: time is an endogenous variable and confounds
1014   both performance and observed values of synergy and redundancy.
1015
1016   We address these issue as follows. First, we focus our analysis on the interaction of synergy and re-
1017
       dundancy, rather than each variable individually to capture the tradeoff between the two. Second, we
       compute both emergent properties (synergy and redundancy) only on early rounds (in our case, 10
1018
       time steps). We chose 10 rounds as a good tradeoff between giving the multi-agent systems enough
1019
       time to evolve, while limiting the amount of runs that have already completed. While this reduces
1020   our ability to detect the full emergent potential of the multi-agent system (since agents have less
1021   time to evolve) it creates a clean apples-to-apples comparison between measured values (they are all
1022   based on exactly 10 time steps). In that sense, our analyses likely underestimate the full effect of
1023   synergy because we only capture the early onset of synergy. Focusing on only the first 10 time steps,
1024   leads to additional complications, however. About 23% of experiments finish early and do not make
1025   it till round ten (roughly balanced across treatments with 22% in Plain, 25% in Persona, and 20% in
       ToM). Simply removing these observations would bias our results since this censoring occurs post-


                                                         19
       Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




1026
       treatment assignment: removing those observations would introduce sampling bias. Instead, we take
1027   these observations as censored (i.e., we cannot compute the early synergy measure) and control for
1028   this censoring using stabilized inverse probability weighting (IPW; Wooldridge, 2002). That is, in
1029   a separate modeling step we predict (via logistic regression and only task difficulty as exogenous
1030   predictor variables that is independent of the treatment) the likelihood of observing (early) synergy
1031   and redundancy.4 We then take the inverse of the predicted likelihood as weight in the causal medi-
1032   ation analysis. This adjust observations in the primary analysis with the inverse of the probability of
1033   making such observations.
1034   Our first analysis is a plain quasibinomial regression on the binary success vs. failure outcome5 with
1035   synergy, redundancy, their interaction, task difficulty as controls, and dummy indicators for the three
1036   treatment conditions. We use our most conservative estimate of synergy and redundancy with the
1037   bias-corrected estimates with high null model sampling (B = 2, 000), and the conservative MMI
1038   redundancy (Mediano et al., 2025) with MI estimated with Miller–Madow bias correction (Miller,
1039   1955). Results are robust to using MI redundancy and Jeffreys-smoothed probabilities.
1040   For our second analysis we perform causal mediation analysis (Imai et al., 2010) to link our inter-
1041   ventions to performance, via multi-agent synergy as a mechanism. We estimate the model using the
1042   {mediation} package in R (Tingley et al., 2014). We find that the ToM intervention has a sig-
1043   nificant indirect effect on performance through its impact on synergy, beyond any direct treatment
1044   effects.
