009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011            Effective communication is crucial for multi-agent cooperation in partially observ-
012            able environments. However, a fundamental trilemma exists among task perfor-
013            mance, communication efficiency, and human interpretability. To resolve this, we
014            propose a multi-agent communication framework via Grounding Language and
015            Contrastive learning (GLC) to learns efficient and interpretable communication
016            protocols. Specifically, GLC employs an autoencoder to learn discretized and
017            compressed communication symbols, ensuring high communication efficiency.
018
               These symbols are then semantically aligned with human concepts using data
               generated by a Large Language Model (LLM), making them human-interpretable.
019
               Furthermore, a contrastive learning objective is introduced to ensure consistency
020
               and mutual intelligibility among all agents, thereby securing high task utility.
021            GLC dynamically balances these objectives by the Information Bottleneck princi-
022            ple. Extensive experiments show that GLC outperforms state-of-the-art methods
023            across multiple benchmarks, delivering superior task performance, higher com-
024            munication efficiency, and enhanced human interpretability.

027   1   I NTRODUCTION
028
029   Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has achieved remarkable success in various complex
030   real-world multi-agent systems (MAS) such as autonomous driving Xu et al. (2024) and traffic signal
031   control Zhang et al. (2024). In MAS under partial observability, communication learning is key to
032   overcoming individual perceptual limitations and achieving coordinated behavior. However, existing
033
      MARL approaches often face a fundamental tension between communication efficiency and human
      interpretability. Methods that prioritize efficiency typically produce opaque protocols that hinder
034
      collaboration with humans Lin et al. (2021); Freed et al. (2020), while those favoring interpretability
035
      often incur high communication costs that limit their practical applicability Lowe et al. (2019).
036
037   This challenge can be understood through the lens of the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle,
038   which formalizes the trade-off between the complexity of a message and its informativeness about
039
      a source variable. As shown in Figure 1(a), in multi-agent settings, this translates to a three-
      way balance among task performance (utility), communication efficiency (complexity), and human
040
      interpretability (informativeness) Tucker et al. (2022a). An ideal protocol must compress high-
041
      dimensional observations into efficient messages (minimizing complexity) while preserving enough
042   information (maximizing informativeness), both to achieve task goals (maximizing utility) and to
043   remain interpretable by humans or unfamiliar agents.
044
045
      To address this trilemma, we propose a multi-agent communication framework via Grounding Lan-
      guage and Contrastive learning (GLC). As shown in Figure 1(b), GLC integrates three key com-
046
      ponents: a discrete autoencoder that minimizes communication complexity through compressed
047
      symbolic representations, a communication alignment mechanism that aligns these discrete sym-
048   bols with human semantic spaces to enhance interpretability, and a contrastive learning objective
049   that ensures consistency and mutual intelligibility among agents. By dynamically balancing these
050   objectives, GLC operationalizes the IB principle for MAS, enabling the emergence of protocols that
051   are simultaneously efficient and interpretable. During execution, agents communicate using com-
052   pact discrete symbols while maintaining rich semantic meaning derived from both LLM supervision
053   and contrastive alignment. Crucially, GLC decouples training-time alignment from deployment-
      time communication: while training utilizes LLM-derived anchors and contrastive learning signals,

080   2   R ELATED W ORK
081
082
      Utility-Optimized Communication. A large body of multi-agent communication work prioritizes
083
      maximizing task performance, often at the expense of interpretability and sometimes efficiency.
084   Early methods like CommNet Sukhbaatar et al. (2016) and IC3Net Singh et al. (2019) enabled end-
085   to-end learning of continuous communication vectors that are highly optimized for specific tasks.
086   Subsequent works like TarMAC Das et al. (2019) and MAGIC Niu et al. (2021) introduced attention
087   mechanisms and targeted communication to enhance utility further. While these approaches excel
088   in performance, the learned protocols are typically opaque and require high communication cost,
089   making them unsuitable for human-agent collaboration or bandwidth-constrained environments.
090   Efficiency-Driven Communication. Another line of research focuses on reducing communication
091   cost, often through discretization Carmeli et al. (2023); Foerster et al. (2016). Methods like aeComm
092   Lin et al. (2021) use autoencoders to compress observations into discrete symbols. VQ-VIB Tucker
093   et al. (2022a) further incorporates IB constraints to learn a constrained vocabulary. These meth-
094   ods achieve high communication efficiency but often result in symbols that are not semantically
095   grounded, limiting their interpretability and generalizability to novel partners.
096   Interpretability-Oriented Communication A more recent trend seeks to align agent communica-
097   tion with human-understandable concepts. Some approaches Lazaridou et al. (2020); Tucker et al.
098   (2022b); Li et al. (2024) leverage pre-trained language models or human data to ground continuous
099   communication vectors in natural language semantics. While these methods enhance interpretabil-
100   ity, they often rely on continuous representations that incur significant communication overhead, or
101   they lack the structured discrete symbols necessary for efficient communication.
102   Bridging the Trilemma. The aforementioned approaches typically excel in one or two dimensions
103   of the trilemma but struggle to balance all three simultaneously. Our proposed GLC framework is
104   designed to bridge this gap. Unlike prior work, GLC integrates discrete symbol learning for effi-
105   ciency, language grounding for interpretability, and contrastive learning for utility and consistency
106   within a unified, information-theoretically motivated framework. It moves beyond the traditional
107   discrete-continuous dichotomy by learning discrete symbols that are embedded in a continuous, se-
      mantically meaningful space, thereby achieving a more favorable balance across all three objectives.

154
      multi-agent communication. As illustrated in Figure 2, the framework integrates four core modules
155
      that work together to address the trilemma involving performance, efficiency, and interpretability:
156   (1) The MARL agent module, which learns policies and generates efficient communication symbols

159   module, which aligns the semantics of discrete symbols with natural language descriptions generated
160   by the LLM; (4) The communication alignement contrastive learning module, which ensures all
161   agents develop a consistent and mutually understandable communication protocol. These modules
      are jointly optimized through a unified multi-objective loss function. Each module will be elaborated

      4.5   DYNAMIC BALANCING OF O BJECTIVES
271
272   The communication protocol is optimized via a multi-objective learning framework integrating four
273   complementary goals: (1) policy gradients that learn to share strategically relevant information
274   across agents; (2) linguistic alignment with human-like communication examples drawn from D; (3)
275   autoencoder-based reconstruction of message semantics; and (4) contrastive learning for communi-
276   cation alignment. These components are combined into a unified optimization objective through the
277   following composite loss:
278
279                          L = Lpolicy + λA Lalign + λR Lrecon + λC Lcontra .                          (5)
280
281   The weighting coefficients λA , λR , and λC balance the contributions of the alignment loss, recon-
282   struction loss, and contrastive loss, respectively. Crucially, instead of treating these as static hy-
283   perparameters, we view them as dynamic controls that guide the emergence of the communication
284   protocol. Inspired by the IB principle’s use of annealing Tishby et al. (2000); Tucker et al. (2022a),
285   we employ a scheduling strategy that initially prioritizes learning a shared, meaningful semantics
286   before gradually increasing pressure for efficient compression. This allows the communication pro-
287
      tocol to evolve adaptively. The scheduling strategy of coefficients is set as follows,
288   (1) Task-Adaptive Alignment λA : The weight λA is set task-dependently. A higher value prioritizes
289   human interpretability in complex tasks like USAR, while a lower value in tasks like Predator-Prey
290   allows greater focus on task performance. (2) Annealing for Communication Efficiency λR : We
291   apply a linear annealing schedule to λR , increasing it from a small value to a higher one. This
292   implements an “explore then compress” strategy: initially allowing rich semantic exploration be-
293
      fore gradually increasing pressure for efficient discrete compression. (3) Stabilizing Consensus λC :
      The λC is kept at a fixed, moderate value to provide a consistent signal that fosters a shared pro-
294
      tocol among agents, ensuring stability during training. In practice, a lightweight scheduler module
295
      updates these weights during training. This dynamic balancing enables GLC to adaptively evolve
296   communication protocols optimized for each task’s specific requirements and learning stage, rather
297   than settling for a static compromise.

458   achieving the task goal with minimal communicative overhead. Finally, the USAR environment,
459   with its heterogeneous roles and complex action sequences, demands a protocol rich in semantic
460   interpretability. Agents must communicate nuanced intentions, requests, and contextual information

462   with human language concepts, sacrificing some raw efficiency for the clarity and unambiguous
463
      understanding required for successful collaboration in such an intricate task. This demonstrates
      that GLC does not seek a single optimal point on the trilemma but rather dynamically adapts its
464

468   6   C ONCLUSIONS
469
470   This paper introduced GLC, a novel multi-agent communication framework that effectively bridges
471
      the long-standing trade-off among task performance, communication efficiency, and human inter-
      pretability. By integrating discrete autoencoder-based compression with LLM-grounded semantic
472
      alignment and inter-agent contrastive learning, GLC enables the emergence of protocols that are
473
      simultaneously communication-efficient, task-effective, and human-interpretable. Crucially, a dy-
474   namic weighting schedule allows the framework to adaptively balance these objectives throughout
475   training, guided by the specific constraints of the task environment. This adaptability ensures the
476   practical applicability of GLC in diverse real-world domains, including robotic swarms where low-
477   bandwidth communication is paramount, and autonomous vehicle fleets where interpretability is
478   crucial for human trust and collaboration.
479
      While GLC represents a significant step forward, several promising directions remain for future
480   work. These include developing dynamic alignment mechanisms that incorporate real-time human
481   feedback, extending the framework to incorporate multimodal signals, and integrating structured
482   semantic constraints or external knowledge graphs to improve the generalization. A deeper theo-
483   retical analysis of the generalization property of emergent communication under grounded learning
484   would also be valuable. Addressing these challenges will further enhance the practicality of GLC
485   for real-world human-AI collaboration and advance the broader goal of developing efficient and
      interpretable multi-agent systems.

596   A.1   I NFORMATION -T HEORETIC F OUNDATION
597
598   The GLC framework is grounded in the IB principle, which formalizes the trade-off between the
599   compression of input data and the preservation of task-relevant information. We frame the learning
600   of communication protocols within this principle: each agent must compress its encoded observa-
601   tion Hi into a concise message Ci , while ensuring that the message remains informative about key
602   variables essential for collaboration. Specifically, the message should be informative about (1) Task
603
      Utility (Y ), to enable effective coordination; (2) Human Interpretability (L), by aligning with seman-
      tic concepts from a language space; and (3) Other Agents’ Perspectives (C−i ), to ensure consistency
604
      and mutual intelligibility.
605
606   The IB objective of maximizing I(Ci ; Y, L, C−i ) while minimizing I(Ci ; Hi ) is intractable to op-
607   timize directly. Instead, GLC operationalizes this principle through a composite loss function that
608   integrates four complementary objectives, each acting as a surrogate for a component of the IB
609
      trade-off:
610
611                           Lpolicy +λA Lalign +λC           Lcontra      +λR Lrecon                   (6)
                                                               | {z }           | {z }
612
                              | {z }      | {z }
                             −I(Ci ;Y )     −I(Ci ;L)        −I(Ci ;C−i )       ≈I(Hi ;Ci )
613
614   Intuitively, the policy loss Lpolicy encourages messages to be informative about task success; the
615   alignment loss Lalign grounds them in human-understandable semantics; the contrastive loss Lcontra
616   fosters consensus among agents; and the reconstruction loss Lrecon regulates the compression pro-
617   cess. The dynamic balancing of coefficients λA , λR , λC allows GLC to adaptively prioritize differ-
618
      ent facets of this trade-off during training. A detailed derivation of the connection between the loss
      function and the IB principle is provided in Appendix A.2.

      A.2   D ETAILED I NFORMATION -T HEORETIC D ERIVATION
621
622   This section provides a detailed derivation of the connection between the GLC loss function and the
623   IB principle. The core IB objective for an agent i can be formulated as finding a communication
624   message Ci that maximizes the following Lagrangian:
625
                                     LIB = I(Ci ; Y, L, C−i ) − βI(Ci ; Hi )                            (7)
626
627
      where Hi = fenc (Oi ) is the encoded representation of observation Oi , I(·; ·) denotes mutual infor-
      mation, and β is a Lagrange multiplier controlling the trade-off.
628
629   This objective is intractable for complex environments. We thus decompose it into tractable surro-
630   gate losses. First, we note that the information preservation term can be lower-bounded by consid-
631   ering the contributions of each target variable individually:
632                          I(Ci ; Y, L, C−i ) ≥ I(Ci ; Y ) + I(Ci ; L) + I(Ci ; C−i )              (8)
633   This simplification allows us to address each information term separately. The GLC loss function
634   (Equation 6) is designed to maximize these terms:
635
                                 −Lpolicy ∝ I(Ci ; Y ) (Task utility)
636
637
                               −λA Lalign ∝ I(Ci ; L) (Human interpretability)
638                           −λC Lcontra ∝ I(Ci ; C−i ) (Inter-agent consistency)
639   Conversely, the compression term I(Ci ; Hi ) is regulated by the reconstruction loss Lrecon .
640
      By enforcing the message Ci to be predictive of the By enforcing the message Ci to be predictive of
641
      the encoded observation Hi via the autoencoder, we implicitly control the complexity of the mes-
642   sage, ensuring that it does not retain excessive, irrelevant information from Hi . Since Hi is itself a
643   compressed representation of Oi obtained through the encoder fenc , controlling I(Ci ; Hi ) effectively
644   constrains I(Ci ; Oi ) through the data processing inequality. Thus, the overall GLC objective can be
645   viewed as a practical and scalable approximation to the idealized IB principle for multi-agent com-
646   munication, where we directly control the information flow through the pathway Oi → Hi → Ci
647   rather than the direct pathway Oi → Ci . This approach maintains the fundamental IB trade-offs
      while remaining tractable for complex environments.

843   The autoencoder is removed; discrete symbol generation is replaced with a direct linear projection

919
920   A.4.1    P REDATOR P REY
921
922   In this environment, n predators with a restricted visual range v collaborate to locate stationary
923   prey within an x × x grid. Agents receive a shared reward when any predator reaches the prey,
924
      and each episode terminates either when all predators have succeeded or after a maximum of T
      timesteps. Each predator perceives only a local v × v grid region and selects movement actions
925
      based on these partial observations, making communication essential for effective coordination. Our
926
      experiments use a 5 × 5 grid with 3 predators and 1 prey under two vision settings: v = 0 and v = 1.
927   Under v = 0, predators perceive the prey only when co-located in the same cell. Each episode
928   is capped at 20 steps. With randomized initial positions and higher-dimensional state and action
929   spaces than standard benchmarks, the Predator-Prey environment presents substantial coordination
930   and perceptual challenges.
931
932   A.4.2    USAR
933
934   The USAR environment Li et al. (2023) simulates a cooperative bomb disposal task in which three
935   specialized agents (Alpha, Bravo, and Charlie) must locate and defuse hidden bombs with unknown
936   color-coded activation sequences. The agents operate in a graph-based environment consisting of n
937
      interconnected rooms. Each agent carries unique wire cutters and can execute three types of actions:
      moving between rooms, inspecting a bomb, or using a cutter. Agents have partial observability,
938
      perceiving only the contents of their current room. The action space is combinatorial in nature,
939
      scaling with the number of rooms (n), available cutters (m), and the inspection action. Defusing a
940   bomb with x phases yields a reward of 10 × x points. Episodes end when all bombs are successfully
941   defused or after a timeout. In our implementation, the environment includes n = 5 rooms and 5
942   bombs with varying difficulty: two 1-phase bombs, two 2-phase bombs, and one 3-phase bomb, each
943   assigned one of three possible colors. Each agent is equipped with two distinct wire cutters. The
944   episode terminates after 100 steps if not completed earlier. The task demands precise coordination,
945   as agents must communicate effectively to share essential information such as bomb sequences and
946   cutter availability.

970   timestep. The interface serves as a bidirectional mediator between the game engine and language
971   model agents, translating states into language and language into actions. It constructs natural lan-
      guage observations by formatting key game elements, such as round number, team score, visible

1001
       To simulate human-agent collaboration under constrained resources, we form mixed teams con-

1008   dataset D. This design enables seamless coordination between learning-based and language-guided

       B    D ISCUSSION ON S CALABILITY AND F UTURE W ORK
1012
1013
       Our selection of the Predator-Prey and USAR environments was strategic, as they serve as estab-
1014   lished and computationally tractable testbeds that effectively capture the core challenges of the
1015   efficiency-utility-interpretability trilemma under study. These environments allowed for the exten-
1016   sive ablation studies and convergence analyses necessary to validate GLC’s core contributions within
1017   practical resource constraints. We acknowledge that evaluation on larger-scale benchmarks like the
1018   StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC) or real-world robotic simulators represents a valuable
1019   direction for future work, and we confirm that the GLC framework is environment-agnostic and
1020   readily generalizable to such scenarios.
1021   The GLC architecture is inherently designed for scalability through several core principles. The
1022   discrete autoencoder ensures bandwidth-efficient communication that is invariant to environment
1023   size or agent population. Furthermore, the contrastive learning objective maintains semantic con-
1024   sistency and protocol coherence across large agent populations by structuring the communication
1025   space based on functional context. The dynamic balancing mechanism, guided by the Informa-
       tion Bottleneck principle, allows the system to adaptively prioritize different objectives—such as


                                                        19
       Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




1026
       compression or semantic richness—depending on the task’s complexity and scale. Our scalability
1027   experiments in Appendix A.3.7, conducted on enlarged grid worlds with increased agent and prey
1028   populations, empirically validate that GLC maintains robust performance and communication effi-
1029   ciency as the problem scale expands. To further demonstrate GLC’s generalization ability, we plan
1030   to test it in more complex embodied settings such as ALFWorld (multi-step reasoning with natural
1031   language) and RoCoBench (grounded multi-agent collaboration). Success in these domains would
1032   strongly validate GLC’s practicality for real-world human-AI collaboration under longer horizons
1033   and physical constraints.
1034   In terms of computational viability, we emphasize that GLC’s design is highly efficient and practical
1035   for real-world deployment. The use of the LLM is strictly confined to a one-time, offline phase for
1036   generating a static dataset of expert trajectories. During the central training and deployment phases,
1037   no LLM queries are made, eliminating any ongoing computational overhead, latency, or cost asso-
1038   ciated with large model inference. This makes GLC particularly suitable for bandwidth-constrained
1039   applications like robotic swarms or autonomous vehicle networks, where both interpretability and
1040   low communication latency are critical.
1041   GLC creates a synergistic relationship with LLMs rather than seeking to replace them. While LLMs
1042   serve as general-purpose knowledge bases and a source of human-aligned semantic grounding, GLC
1043   learns task-specific, highly efficient communication protocols. Our ad-hoc teamwork experiments
1044   demonstrate that these two paradigms can interoperate effectively, with GLC agents successfully
1045   collaborating in mixed teams with LLM agents. This shows that GLC’s protocols are not only effi-
1046   cient but also semantically accessible to external human-like intelligences, bridging the gap between
1047
       opaque RL protocols and verbose natural language.
1048   Looking ahead, our future work will explicitly explore GLC’s application in more complex and
1049   demanding domains. This includes application to extended multi-agent benchmarks like SMAC,
1050   investigation into distributed training strategies to handle increased environmental complexity, and
1051   deeper analysis of how the emergent communication vocabulary and its syntactic structure evolve
1052   with task difficulty. We are confident that the GLC framework provides a solid and scalable founda-
1053
       tion for these future research directions toward practical and interpretable multi-agent systems.
