009                                              A BSTRACT
010
011            With an ever-growing zoo of LLMs and benchmarks, the need to orchestrate mul-
012            tiple models for improved task performance has never been more pressing. While
013            frameworks like Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) attempt to coordinate LLMs, they of-
014            ten fall short in terms of (1) selecting relevant agents, (2) facilitating effective
015            intra-agent communication, and (3) integrating responses efficiently. In this work,
016            we propose Graph-of-Agents (GoA), a new graph-based framework for model-
017
               ing multi-agent LLM communication. Our approach begins with node sampling,
               selecting only the most relevant agents by leveraging model cards that summa-
018
               rize each model’s domain, task specialization, and other characteristics. Next, we
019
               construct edges between the selected agents by evaluating their responses against
020            one another to determine relevance ordering. Directed message passing is then
021            performed from highly relevant agents to less relevant ones to enhance their re-
022            sponses, followed by reverse message passing to refine the original responses
023            of the more relevant agents. Finally, the updated responses are aggregated via
024            graph-based pooling (e.g., max or mean pooling) to produce a single, unified an-
025            swer. We evaluate GoA on diverse multi-domain benchmarks (MMLU, MMLU-
026            Pro, GPQA) and domain-specific benchmarks (MATH, HumanEval, MedM-
027            CQA), with an agent pool of 6 LLMs spanning multiple domains. Surpris-
028
               ingly, GoA achieves superior performance using only 3 selected agents, outper-
               forming recent multi-agent LLM baselines that utilize all 6 agents simultane-
029
               ously. By adopting a graph structure, GoA offers both scalability and effectiveness
030
               through structured message passing—positioning it as a strong candidate for nav-
031            igating the challenges of the ever-growing LLM zoo. The source code of GoA can
032            be found at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/goa-F23C.

      Too many LLMs, too many benchmarks. As the ecosystem of Large Language Models (LLMs) (Wei
037   et al., 2022a) rapidly diversifies, researchers are increasingly overwhelmed—not just by the sheer
038   number of models available, but also by the complexity of evaluating and combining them effectively
039   at test time to solve complex tasks. In this era of abundant LLMs, a central challenge emerges:
040
041       (Q) Given the diversity of available LLMs, how can we design an effective playground where
042
          agents interact synergistically—leveraging strengths, compensating for weaknesses, and im-
          proving decision-making through efficient collaboration?
043
044   As an early attempt to address this challenge, Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) (Wang et al., 2024a; Li
045   et al., 2025) has recently been introduced as a pioneering approach that explores how leveraging mul-
046   tiple LLMs can enhance overall model performance through the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) (Shazeer
047   et al., 2017) framework. As illustrated in Figure 1 (c), MoA aggregates responses from multiple
048   LLM agents, appends them to the original query and feeds the enriched input to the next layer. This
049   facilitates multi-agent synergy, enabling models to complement each other and refine predictions
050
      collaboratively. While MoA has shown that integrating multiple LLMs can be advantageous over a
      single model, it still faces several limitations that hinder its scalability and effectiveness:
051
052   Which agents? As shown in Figure 1 (b), following the scaling law of diverse LLM agents, se-
053   lecting a relevant subset from a large pool of agents handling complex and diverse queries (Figure 1
      (a)) is a crucial challenge. The current MoA approach lacks an effective agent selection mecha-

      How do they communicate? Once agents are selected, facilitating effective communication among
077
      them becomes a pivotal challenge. MoA adopts a many-to-one aggregation scheme, but this ap-
078   proach has notable drawbacks: it requires collecting responses from all available agents and treating
079   them as a single chunk, which fails to capture fine-grained interactions—such as one-to-one commu-
080   nication between individual agent pairs. Moreover, since different agents have varying strengths and
081   relevance depending on the query, treating all agent messages equally can hinder consensus. Instead,
082   adaptively weighting responses based on their relevance is crucial for improving decision-making.
083
      How to integrate? Finally, when making the final decision, MoA aggregates responses by con-
084   catenating tokens from all agents. However, this approach incurs a huge computational cost, with
085   a complexity of O(LN d), where L is the number of communication layers, N is the number of
086   agents, and d is the token length per agent. Given that token usage is directly tied to cost, this
087   method becomes prohibitively expensive. Moreover, not all agents contribute equally valuable re-
088   sponses—some domain-specialized agents produce significantly higher-quality outputs than others.
089   A scalable integration mechanism that prioritizes more reliable agents while reducing the impact of
090   less relevant ones is essential for cost-efficient and effective decision-making.
091   ⋆ Our Approach. To address these challenges, we propose a graph-based multi-agent framework,
092   Graph-of-Agents (GoA), as a novel remedy to enhance multi-agent communication. GoAfunda-
093   mentally rethinks multi-agent collaboration by modeling agents as nodes and their relevance-based
094   relationships as edges, enabling structured message passing. This graph-based design allows for the
095   selective activation of only relevant agents (as a subgraph of entire pool) while capturing inter-agent
096   interactions through message passing and finalizing decisions via graph-based pooling. GoA follows
097   a structured process, as shown in Figure 1 (d):
098   ❶ Which agents? → Node Sampling: GoA begins by selecting relevant agents based on available
099   metadata (e.g., domain, task) from model cards. This information is provided to a meta-LLM, simply
100   a general-domain LLM, which identifies the most relevant agents given the query.
101
      ❷ How do they communicate? → Edge Sampling & Message Passing: Once nodes (agents) are
102   selected, we obtain their initial responses and ask each agent to rank others, capturing the signifi-
103   cance of each agent’s output. Based on these rankings, we construct directed edges in two perspec-
104   tives: (i) Source-to-Target: Higher-ranked agents (i.e., highly influential nodes with more relevant
105   responses) propagate their information to lower-ranked agents, allowing them to refine their re-
106   sponses based on more confident initial answers. (ii) Target-to-Source: After lower-ranked agents
107   update their responses, the refined information is passed back to the higher-ranked agents, enabling
      them to further adjust their outputs based on the improved responses from their neighborhood agents.

119
120   • We identify key challenges in current multi-agent LLM systems: selecting which agents to sample,

122   •   We formulate multi-agent collaboration as a graph-based framework and introduce GoA, which

      2     R ELATED W ORK
133
134   LLM Reasoning and Test-Time Inference. Test-time reasoning with LLMs has seen rapid ad-
135   vances through prompt engineering techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022b),
136
      Tree-of-Thought (Yao et al., 2023), and Graph-of-Thought (Besta et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024),
      which enable models to decompose complex problems into structured sub-tasks. While most prior
137
      work focuses on improving a single LLM’s reasoning via internal prompting strategies (Zhou et al.,
138
      2023; Xu et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024), a parallel direction has emerged around collaborative rea-
139   soning at inference time using multiple LLMs (Du et al., 2023a; Chan et al., 2023). In these multi-
140   agent setups, multiple LLMs interact in test-time without additional finetuning, often aiming to
141   boost factual accuracy or reasoning diversity. However, these approaches typically rely on simplis-
142   tic communication protocols such as symmetric debate or sequential refinement, lacking structured
143   mechanisms for message routing or adaptive collaboration. Our work builds on this foundation
144   by proposing a graph-based test-time collaboration framework that formalizes agent interactions
145   through directional message passing, enabling richer and more scalable multi-agent reasoning.
146   LLM Ensembles and Multi-Agent LLM Collaboration. Another approach to leveraging mul-
147   tiple LLMs is ensemble-based inference, where outputs from several models are aggregated or se-
148   lected (Fang et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2023). Recent works introduce router mechanisms (Wang et al.,
149   2023c; Hari & Thomson, 2023; Wang et al., 2024b; Yue et al., 2025) to reduce computational cost by
150   selectively querying a subset of models. However, many of these ensemble strategies treat LLMs as
151   interchangeable units without modeling their relationships. Meanwhile, graph-based structures have
152   begun to emerge to coordinate multi-agent systems, notably in frameworks like MacNet (Qian et al.,
153
      2024) and GPTSwarm (Zhuge et al., 2024), which model agents as nodes in static DAGs. Also
      DyLAN (Liu et al., 2024b) performs dynamic agent activation using an Agent Importance Score
154
      computed via forward–backward peer-rating propagation and coordinates agents through a tempo-
155
      ral feed-forward communication network. Yet, these approaches often rely on predefined topologies
156   or assume a single agent role-playing multiple personas, limiting flexibility and expressiveness, and
157   sometimes even requiring additional optimization.. In contrast, our proposed framework, Graph-
158   of-Agents (GoA), constructs dynamic graphs based on task relevance, enabling one-to-one com-
159   munication across a diverse pool of specialized LLMs. Through node sampling, directional edge
160   construction, and graph pooling, GoA supports efficient and adaptive collaboration—addressing the
161   underexplored challenge of coordinating heterogeneous agents for domain-diverse reasoning at test
      time.

190   3.1                        P RELIMINARIES AND N OTATIONS
191
192   Multi-agent LLMs. We define a system of N LLM agents, where each agent i ∈ 1, . . . , N special-
193   izes in a particular domain or task. The agents collaboratively process a given query Q to generate
194   an optimized response A.
195   As a graph. We represent the multi-agent system as a directed graph, G = (V, E), where V =
196   v1 , . . . , vN denotes the set of agent nodes and E ⊆ V × V is the directed edges. Once a subset of S
197   relevant agents are selected from the multi-agent pool of size N , the adjacency matrix A ∈ RS×S
198   is constructed. The matrix is defined such that Aij > 0 if (vi , vj ) ∈ E and Aij = 0 otherwise.
199
200   3.2                        O UR APPORACH : G OA
201
      In this section, we present GoA, a novel approach specifically designed to enhance multi-agent
202
      communication through a graph-based framework, as shown in Figure 2.
203
204   Overview. GoA begins with node sampling, where a meta-LLM (a general-purpose LLM) selects
205   the most relevant agents from a given pool based on the query and a model card dictionary. Each
206   model card summarizes information extracted from the Hugging Face README file, including the
207
      LLM’s domain and specialized tasks (Sec. 3.2.1). After collecting initial responses from the selected
      agents, we rank each agent based on scores assigned by other agents. Agents are then sorted by
208
      these scores to define two node types: source nodes (highly relevant and influential) and target
209
      nodes (less influential). This establishes bidirectional relationships: source-to-target and target-to-
210   source (Sec. 3.2.2). In the message-passing, we first propagate messages from source to target,
211   allowing target nodes to refine their responses based on input from more relevant agents. We then
212   reverse the flow—target to source—so that source nodes can further refine their outputs using the
213   updated responses. This two-step process ensures that each agent’s contribution is weighted by its
214   relevance to the query (Sec. 3.2.3). Finally, we apply graph pooling: either max pooling based on the
215   most connected source node or mean pooling guided by the meta-LLM to generate the final answer
      (Sec. 3.2.4).

       E      L IMITATION AND F UTURE W ORK
996
997    While GoA demonstrates strong performance in multi-agent collaboration, several limitations re-
998    main. First, the current node sampling mechanism primarily relies on publicly available model card
999    information to select relevant agents. However, model card descriptions may be incomplete, incon-
1000
       sistent, or lack crucial details about a model’s strengths and weaknesses in specific domains. As a
       result, the selection process may not always optimally align with the query requirements. Future
1001
       work could explore more adaptive selection mechanisms, such as meta-learning-based optimiza-
1002
       tion or reinforcement learning techniques, to dynamically refine agent selection based on historical
1003   performance and contextual relevance.
1004
1005   Moreover, GoA primarily focuses on text-based reasoning tasks, leveraging structured communica-
1006
       tion among language models. However, many real-world applications require multimodal reasoning,
       where agents process and integrate textual, visual, and numerical information simultaneously. Ex-
1007
       tending GoA to multimodal domains by incorporating vision-language models, structured numerical
1008
       reasoning, or even cross-modal knowledge fusion would be a promising direction.
1009
1010   Lastly, while the current benchmarks used in our study (e.g., MMLU, GPQA, MedMCQA) are
1011   not interactive environments, our framing is consistent with the Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) line of
1012
       work—both conceptually (multiple LLMs collaborating as an agent system, illustrated in Figure
       1 of the main paper) and in evaluation setup, which similarly relies on question–answering tasks
1013
       (e.g., AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-Bench, FLASK) to study test-time agent collaboration. That said, in fu-
1014
       ture work, we plan to explore more interactive, environment-grounded agentic benchmarks, such as
1015   WebAgent and GUI navigation. Incorporating GoA into such settings—where agents interact with
1016   tools, execute actions, or access the web—would require adapting the agent pool and communica-
1017   tion protocols, and is indeed a promising direction for future work. In particular, GoA’s graph-based
1018   construction could help integrate heterogeneous tool responses into a coherent final answer, some-
1019   what analogous to GPTSwarm’s application of Self-Consistency.
