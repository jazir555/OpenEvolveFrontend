                                              Abstract
009
              Accurate prediction of human behavior is essential for robust and safe
010
              human-AI collaboration. However, existing approaches for modeling peo-
011
              ple are often data-hungry and brittle because they either make unrealistic
012           assumptions about rationality or are too computationally demanding to
013           adapt rapidly. Our key insight is that many everyday social interactions may
014           follow predictable patterns; efficient “scripts” that minimize cognitive load
015           for actors and observers, e.g., “wait for the green light, then go.” We propose
016           modeling these routines as behavioral programs instantiated in computer
017           code rather than policies conditioned on beliefs and desires. We introduce
018           ROTE, a novel algorithm that leverages both large language models (LLMs)
019           for synthesizing a hypothesis space of behavioral programs, and probabilistic
020
              inference for reasoning about uncertainty over that space. We test ROTE
              in a suite of gridworld tasks and a large-scale embodied household simu-
021
              lator. ROTE predicts human and AI behaviors from sparse observations,
022
              outperforming competitive baselines—including behavior cloning and LLM-
023           based methods—by as much as 50% in terms of in-sample accuracy and
024           out-of-sample generalization. By treating action understanding as a program
025           synthesis problem, ROTE opens a path for AI systems to efficiently and
026           effectively predict human behavior in the real-world.

029   1   Introduction
030
      Predicting the behavior of others (Theory of Mind) is a core challenge for building intelligent
031   social agents. Whether anticipating a pedestrian’s movements, coordinating with teammates,
032   or interacting safely in public spaces, machines must infer what others are likely to do next.
033   Existing approaches such as behavior cloning (BC) and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL)
034   rely on learning models to predict low-level actions or infer latent reward functions (Abbeel &
035   Ng, 2004; Ng et al., 2000; Torabi et al., 2018; Wulfmeier et al., 2016). However, these methods
036   are often data-hungry and brittle because they try to learn what an agent might do in every
037   possible state, frequently overfitting to specific environments or overcomplicating behaviors
038   that are surprisingly routine for humans (Skalse & Abate, 2024; Yildirim et al., 2024).
039
      Alternatively, probabilistic methods for goal inference (Fuchs et al., 2023; Zhi-Xuan et al.,
      2020; 2024) are more sample efficient but demand computationally intensive online reasoning
040
      about potential intentions and beliefs, alongside human-specified priors and hypothesis
041
      spaces. Thus, conventional methods for modeling others present a trade-off illustrated in
042   Figure 1: data-intensive and brittle, or compute-intensive and manually constructed for each
043   new domain.
044
045
      Recent work in cognitive science shows that when humans interact with one another, we do
      not always imbue others with deeply held mental states such as goals or beliefs. Instead we
046
      often perceive others as following a script or mindlessly applying a set of rules (Ullman &
047
      Bass, 2024; Bass et al., 2024). For example, when someone steps into a crosswalk, we do
048   not need to infer their ultimate destination, their complex mental states, or their opinion on
049   pineapple on pizza. It is enough to apply a commonly understood “crosswalk script” shaped
050   by social convention. While there are perspectives on how people adopt roles in societies or
051   prescribe agency to others (Dennett, 1972; Field, 1978; Dennett & Gorey, 1981; Dennett,
052   1987; 2017; Jara-Ettinger & Dunham, 2024), to the best of our knowledge, there are currently
053   no computational models that adequately describe how machines can represent and reason
      about other agents acting in a script-like manner.

      The notion of representing an intelligent agent through logical rules and predetermined
      decision-making processes is a foundational idea in computer science (Newell & Simon, 1956;
074
      Schank & Abelson, 2013; Newell & Simon, 1976), influencing fields from planning (Campbell
075
      et al., 2002; Zhu et al., 2025) to game theory (Axelrod, 1980). Finite State Machines (FSMs),
076   for instance, are still used in video games to efficiently simulate large numbers of agents.
077   By defining a sequence of states and transitions (e.g., patrol border → find agents → chase
078   agents), code can flexibly model the causal behaviors underpinning social norms and routines.
079
      Here we develop ROTE — Representing Others’ Trajectories as Executables — a novel
080
      algorithm that leverages LLMs as code synthesis tools to predict others’ actions. We prompt
081
      LLMs to generate computer programs explaining observed behavioral traces, then perform
082   Bayesian inference to reason about which programs are most likely. This gives us a dynamic
083   representation that can be analyzed, modified, and composed across agents and environments.
084
085
      ROTE significantly improves generalization and efficiency in predicting complex agent
      behavior, showing up to a 50% increase in accuracy across multiple challenging embodied
086
      domains. Our results in gridworlds and the scaled-up Partnr household robotics simulator
087
      demonstrate that code is a highly effective representation for modeling and predicting
088   behavior. To validate its applicability to real-world complexity, we collected human gameplay
089   data and found that our method achieves human-level accuracy in predicting human actions,
090   outperforming all baselines. This offers a promising new path for creating scalable, adaptable,
091   and interpretable socially intelligent AI systems. Concretely, our contributions are:
092
      1. Modeling Agentic Behavior via Program Synthesis: We develop ROTE, a novel
093      algorithm that combines LLMs with Sequential Monte Carlo to model other agents’
094      behavior as programs from sparse observations.
095
      2. Superior, Scalable Action Prediction: Across two embodied domains, we show
096      that ROTE offers superior generalization for predicting others’ behaviors, outperforming
097      alternative methods by as much as 50%. Our method generates executable code that is
098      reusable across environments, bypassing costly reasoning over goals and beliefs. These
099      code-based representations scale more efficiently than behavior cloning or inverse planning
100      alternatives, even when the ground truth behavior does not come from a known program.
101   3. Human Studies Validation: We recruit real human participants to generate behavior
102      and predict others’ actions. We find that ROTE outperforms baselines and achieves human-
103      level performance in predicting human behaviors, even for noisy and sparse trajectories.

      2    Related Work
106
107   Action Prediction. Prior work developing AI for action prediction follows two dominant
      categories: symbolic methods and neural networks. Symbolic methods, such as Bayesian

      Inverse Planning (BIP), infer an agent’s goals and beliefs by calculating their probabilities
109   based on observed actions (Ullman et al., 2009; Baker et al., 2017; Shum et al., 2019;
110   Netanyahu et al., 2021; Kleiman-Weiner et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020; Kleiman-Weiner et al.,
111   2020; Serrino et al., 2019; Kleiman-Weiner et al., 2025). While robust, these methods are not
112   scalable due to the exponential complexity of a multi-agent environment (Rathnasabapathy
113   et al., 2006; Doshi & Gmytrasiewicz, 2009; Seaman et al., 2018). In contrast, neural
114   approaches like behavioral cloning (BC) and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) train
115   models to directly mimic actions (Torabi et al., 2018; Ng et al., 2000; Abbeel & Ng, 2004;
116   Wulfmeier et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021; Christiano et al., 2023), but are often data-intensive,
117
      fragile, and prone to overfitting. Recent work has tried modeling reward functions as finite-
      state automatons, a concept known as “reward machines” (Icarte et al., 2018; Toro Icarte
118
      et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025). This method, which does not use LLMs, allows for structured
119
      representation of reward and can provide non-Markovian feedback to agents. While primarily
120   used for training agents to solve compositional tasks, there has been work on inferring
121   reward machines from expert demonstrations (Zhou & Li, 2022) or learning safety constraints
122   (Malik et al., 2021; Lindner et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Despite these advances, neural
123   models still struggle with generalization, particularly in social reasoning, as they often fail to
124   capture the causal structure of behavior (de Haan et al., 2019; Codevilla et al., 2019; Bain &
125   Sammut, 1995). This brittleness persists even with advanced techniques that learn contextual
126   representations (Rabinowitz et al., 2018; Chuang et al., 2020; Jha et al., 2024) and does not
127   disappear at scale under an assumption of imperfect rationality (Poddar et al., 2024). In
128
      contrast, our approach, which uses an LLM to generate open-ended code describing observed
      behavior, makes fewer assumptions about the nature of the agents being modeled. This
129
      allows it to capture everyday decision-making processes that may not be reward-maximizing.
130
131   Large Language Models (LLMs) for Behavior Modeling. LLMs may be a more
132   effective bridge between the neural and symbolic paradigms. They enable enumerative
133   inference for social reasoning (Wilf et al., 2023; Jung et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Jin
134
      et al., 2024; Cross et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), while neuro-symbolic
      frameworks (e.g., BIP + LLMs) improve robustness in embodied cooperation (Ying et al.,
135
      2024; Ding et al., 2024; Ying et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025; Castro et al., 2025; Zhou et al.,
136
      2024; Qiu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2024). However, existing implementations
137   remain computationally intensive, often generating thousands of tokens for each prediction.
138   In realistic settings, we need methods capable of rapid inference that still capture the
139   structure of culturally shaped conventions and behaviors performed without deep cognitive
140   processing (Bargh, 1994; Wood, 2024). By learning a code-based agent representation, ROTE
141   avoids the high computational cost that BIP must incur to enumerate every possible goal.
142
      Program Induction. Program synthesis has proven effective for world modeling (Guan
143   et al., 2023; Wong et al., 2023b;a; Zhu & Simmons, 2024), action selection (Verma et al.,
144   2021; Wang et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023), and has even achieved near-expert performance on
145   mathematical reasoning tasks such as International Math Olympiad problems (Trinh et al.,
146   2024). Neurosymbolic approaches, which combine LLMs or domain-specific neural networks
147   with probabilistic program inference, have enabled agents to learn environment dynamics
148   (Das et al., 2023) and master complex games like Sokoban and Frostbite with impressive
149   sample efficiency (Tang et al., 2024; Tsividis et al., 2021; Tomov et al., 2023). Code-like
150   representations have been used to infer reward functions from state-action transitions (Yu
151
      et al., 2023; Davidson et al., 2025), and LLMs have been harnessed to synthesize policies
      or planning strategies in domain-specific contexts (Liang et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023;
152
      Trivedi et al., 2022). However, these prior approaches typically rely on well-defined rewards,
153
      domain-specific constraints, or focus on partial aspects of agent behavior, such as reward
154   inference or demonstration summarization. In contrast, ROTE aims to infer an agent’s
155   causal decision-making process directly from observed behavior and assumes no access to
156   reward signals or domain-specific structure.

      3.1   Agent Program Synthesis with Large Language Models
193
194   Given a finite length history h0:t−1 ∈ H, from time 0 to t − 1, our objective is to find an
195   agent λ̂ ∈ Λ that both (1) takes the same action at as the ground truth agent λ∗ when
196   presented with observation ot , and (2) minimizes its program size |λ̂|. Encouraging concise
197   program synthesis is not just a matter of engineering preference but is theoretically grounded
198   in the foundations of algorithmic probability and inductive inference. Solomonoff’s theory of
199   inductive inference formalizes Occam’s razor, demonstrating that the best scientific model
200   for a given set of observations is the shortest algorithm (in terms of description length) that
201
      generates the data in question (Solomonoff, 1964; 1978; 1996). Under this framework, shorter
      programs are assigned higher prior probability, providing a universal solution to the problem
202
      of induction with strong convergence guarantees: the expected cumulative prediction error is
203
      bounded by the Kolmogorov complexity of the true data-generating process (Solomonoff,
204   1978; 1996). Thus, searching for minimal agent representations is not only computationally
205   desirable but also theoretically optimal for generalization, a bias also observed in human
206   programmatic reasoning (Bigelow & Ullman, 2025).
207
      We operationalize our search through the space of agents Λ with a two-stage approach: First,
208
      we optionally prompt an LLM to transform raw perceptual inputs into a natural language
209
      description of an agent’s path. These percepts can be low-level observations like object
210   coordinates in gridworlds, or even natural language scene-graphs from datasets like Partnr
211   (Chang et al., 2025). Next, we have the LLM generate many possible Python programs
212   to obtain a distribution over possible code-based agent models which explain the observed
213   behavior, ∆(Λ). Python is chosen for its readability, widespread use in AI research, and its
214   power as a Turing-complete language, enabling the representation of arbitrarily complex
215   decision-making logic in the worst case where |S| = |O| for the ground-truth agent λ∗ . Our
      prompting strategy makes two key assumptions: (1) the observed agent follows deterministic

      transitions between finite internal states S contingent on environmental/historical cues
217   rather than executing complex adaptive policies, and (2) generated code should produce
218   deterministic actions a ∈ A. Importantly, we ask the LLM to assume these properties of the
219   observed trajectories even if the ground truth agent generating the behavior is probabilistic
220   and following sophisticated, goal-directed plans. While this assumes a deterministic agent, we
221   account for potential stochasticity in behavior with a noise model, allowing our approach
222   to best approximate the underlying deterministic policy. We instruct the LLM to generate
223   code that is efficient (low runtime complexity) and concise (minimize |λ|).

228   Monte Carlo. Specifically, we estimate the posterior probability of a candidate agent program

      Environments. We evaluate ROTE across two distinct environments. First, we use
      Construction (shown in Figure 7), a fully-observable 2D grid-world where agents actively
256
      navigate obstacles like walls and other agents, and can transport colored blocks to different
257
      locations on the map (Jha et al., 2024). Then, we explore the efficacy of our method on

268   to generate open-ended hypotheses about an agent’s beliefs, goals, and desires, then applies
269   Bayesian Inverse Planning to find the most likely action. Naive LLM (NLLM). NLLM
      simply prompts an LLM with observed states and environment dynamics to predict the next

336       5                   Results
337
          How well does ROTE model and predict scripted agent behavior? To evaluate
338       the effectiveness of ROTE, we first examined its predictive accuracy in a controlled setting
339       where agents in the Construction environment followed one of 10 handcrafted programs.
340       These programs were not provided to ROTE at any point during evaluation. Our results in
341       Figure 3a demonstrate that ROTE consistently surpasses all baselines in both single-step and
342       multi-step prediction accuracy in this evaluation setting and does not statistically significantly
343       underperform human performance (p=0.3087 for single-step and p=0.1679 for multi-step in
344       a two-sided t-test). While these initial results were promising, a potential concern was that
345       ROTE might simply be exploiting repetitive patterns, rather than learning the underlying
346
          policy. We investigated this by measuring how often an agent revisited a state or repeated
          an action. We found an extremely low correlation between ROTE’s accuracy and either of
347
          these metrics (0.303 for matching states, 0.064 for matching actions), confirms that ROTE is
348
          not exploiting simple data regularities. This finding, paired with ROTE’s strong multi-step
349       performance, suggests that code-based representations can be effective for learning the
350       underlying policies that enable robust, long-term predictions.
351
352
          How well does ROTE model and predict human behavior? Having established that
          ROTE’s code-based representations are effective in controlled, scripted environments, we next
353

392   Generalizing to Novel Environments: A key advantage of modeling behavior with
393   scripts is the potential for rapid generalization to new, but similar, environments. We wanted
394
      to know if ROTE’s inferred programs could transfer without needing to be relearned. To
      test this, we first observed a scripted agent following a pattern like “patrol counterclockwise”
395
      for 20 timesteps, and then showed the same agent in a distinct environment. We then asked
396
      ROTE and the baselines to predict the next 10 actions of the same agent. For ROTE, this
397   was done by using the same set of programs inferred in the first environment for prediction
398   without updating their likelihoods. Figure 4 shows that ROTE can still predict the agent’s
399   behavior accurately in the new setting, outperforming all baselines without needing to
400   re-incur the cost of text generation, a necessary step for NLLM and AutoToM. Although
401   ROTE’s performance decreases compared to predicting behavior in the original environment
402   in Figure 3a, its ability to generalize makes it a more accurate and efficient alternative to
403   traditional Inverse Planning or purely neural methods.
404   Can ROTE’s code-based approach scale to model behavior in complex, realistic
405   environments? To further push the boundaries of ROTE’s capabilities, we tested it on the
406   embodied robotics benchmark Partnr, where the task is to predict the next tool utilized by
407   LLM-agents simulating a human or robot completing chores. This environment is particularly
408   challenging due to partial observability and long-horizon, compositional tasks such as “find a
409   plate and clean it in the kitchen” or “look for toys and organize them neatly in the bedroom.”
410   Despite this complexity, Figure 5 shows our approach significantly outperformed all baselines,
411
      including inverse planning and behavior cloning methods, and those incorporating LLMs. To
      better understand the types of problems ROTE excels at, we used Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct to
412
      cluster the ground-truth tasks from our test set into three categories, as shown in Figure 10.
413
      While baselines like AutoToM and Behavior Cloning showed success with tasks involving
414   simple navigation, ROTE demonstrated a superior ability to handle more intricate problems,
415   such as turning items on/off and cleaning objects. This demonstrates its generalizability in
416   creating code for agents that face uncertainty and possess beliefs about their environment.
417
      How does the computational efficiency of ROTE compare to other approaches?
418
      Forming long-horizon plans in the presence of other agents requires predicting their behaviors
419
      over time quickly and not just accurately. To understand whether ROTE scales effectively,
420   we plot the time in seconds required for different baselines to make predictions about agents’
421   behaviors multiple times into the future in Construction. As shown in Figure 6, while ROTE
422   is initially slower for single-step predictions compared to BC and NLLM baselines due to the
423   need to generate and prune candidate programs, its test-time compute costs scale orders of
424   magnitude more efficiently with the number of predictions. This is because once ROTE’s
425   code-based representations are inferred, it can execute these programs rapidly for all future
426   steps. In contrast, other LLM-based methods must re-generate a response for every new
427   time step. We analyze additional factors contributing to this efficiency in Appendix A.5 and
428
      Figure 14. Taken together with the results from Figures 3, 4, and 5, this illustrates that
      code-based representations can balance predictive power with prediction efficiency.
429
430   What is the relationship between ROTE’s core components and its predictive
431   performance? To understand how ROTE achieves its superior generalization and human-
      level accuracy, we conducted a series of ablation studies on its core components. We found


                                                                          8
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




432
      that ROTE’s two-stage observation parsing, which converts observations into a natural
433   language description before generating code, had a minimal effect on accuracy for the FSM
434   and human gameplay datasets in Construction (Figure 11). However, this process significantly
435   hurt performance in Partnr. This is likely because Partnr’s observations are already rich
436   scene graphs (Chang et al., 2025), and the abstraction step removes crucial details needed
437   for effective program generation. Additionally, we investigated the use of Sequential Monte
438   Carlo (SMC) with rejuvenation versus standard Importance Sampling. SMC, which replaces
439   low-likelihood programs with new ones, improved early-stage accuracy when the number of
440   sampled hypotheses was small (Figure 12). This benefit, however, diminished as the initial
441
      set of candidate hypotheses increased, suggesting that the initial diversity provided by the
      LLM is often sufficient.
442
443   Lastly, we analyzed the impact of imposing different degrees of structural constraints on
444   ROTE’s program generation, inspired by methods for inferring reward functions (Yu et al.,
445   2023). We evaluated three variants: “Light” (assuming agents are FSMs without providing
446   examples), “Moderate” (defining FSM states explicitly but allowing open-ended code), and
447
      “Severe” (a two-stage process converting natural language predictions of FSMs into code).
      Our previous results were based on the “Light” condition. The optimal level of structure,
448
      however, varied by environment, as shown in Figure 13. In the Construction environment,
449
      where agents followed predictable FSMs, the Severe approach performed as well as others.
450   This suggests that for predictable, rote behaviors, an explicitly structured representation can
451   be just as effective while also being computationally efficient (Callaway et al., 2018; Lieder
452   & Griffiths, 2020; Callaway et al., 2022; Icard, 2023). Conversely, modeling human behavior
453   proved less suited for strict FSMs. The Moderate condition was superior for human gameplay,
454   highlighting the need for representational flexibility when agents are following a general script
455   but exhibiting inherent variability. In the partially observable Partnr environment, forcing
456   agents into a strict FSM representation performed significantly worse than open-ended code
457   generation, suggesting these scenarios might be better suited for traditional Inverse Planning
458
      methods that can handle a wider range of states and tasks. These findings reveal a gradient of
      agentic representations, from automatic to goal-directed, which allows for flexible prediction
459
      across different scenarios. Future work could use meta-reinforcement learning to dynamically
460
      select the appropriate level of representational structure based on the task.
461

      6    Discussion
464   In this work, we framed behavior inference as a program synthesis problem, showing that
465   our approach, ROTE, can accurately and efficiently predict the actions of machines and real
466   people in complex environments. ROTE offers a scalable alternative to traditional methods
467   that require extensive datasets or significant computational resources. This has immediate
468   implications for domains where real-time adaptability and interpretability are crucial, such
469   as with caregiver robots that could use ROTE’s representations to anticipate daily routines.
470   Limitations: While our results highlight the effectiveness of program synthesis for text-
471
      based observations, we note the limitations of the applicability of our findings in Partnr since
      we only predicted high-level tools used by agents, which was done to accommodate baselines
472
      which required static action spaces. While our evaluation in Partnr still involved more
473
      tools than our other experiments (19 actions in Partnr compared to 6 in the Construction),
474   future research should explore ROTE in high-dimensional, continuous control settings. In
475   those cases, ROTE might need to be integrated with vision-language models (VLMs) to
476   parse pixel-based inputs (e.g., raw video feeds for assistive robots) and neural control
477   mechanisms to execute plans, effectively operating at the level of option prediction (Sutton
478   et al., 1999). Another interesting direction would be to explore how the size of LLMs used
479   for behavioral program inference impacts prediction quality in more sophisticated scenarios,
480   such as modeling team coordination in workplaces or norm enforcement on social platforms.
481   Lastly, unlike traditional Theory of Mind approaches that predict beliefs and goals, our
482   work focuses solely on action prediction. If we view beliefs as dispositions to act (Ramsey &
483   Moore, 1927; Ryle, 1949), predicting a distribution over an agent’s internal decision-making
484   states and logic for transitioning between them is functionally equivalent to belief inference.
485   ROTE is designed to excel in scenarios dominated by predictable, routine, or script-like
      behaviors, such as daily routines in warehouses and stores, relatively stable social conventions


                                                     9
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




486
      like driving, or routine household settings. This is because ROTE exploits the efficiency
487   of executing simple code for long-horizon prediction in these routine settings. For ROTE
488   to gain true generality and address the rigidity concern, future work is explicitly focused
489   on extending it to generate Probabilistic Programming Languages (PPLs), such as memo,
490   which is specialized for social reasoning in JAX (Chandra et al., 2025). This extension would
491   allow ROTE to infer the distribution over actions or latent mental states, directly addressing
492   the stochastic nature of human actions without abandoning the executable code format. In
493   terms of failure modes, domains requiring high-fidelity continuous control over raw sensor
494   data (e.g., video feeds) require ROTE’s inferred high-level programs to be integrated into a
495
      Task and Motion Planning architecture, where ROTE provides the symbolic task plan to
      a low-level neural control mechanism. Finally, for deeply complex, goal-directed behaviors
496
      involving “unknown unknowns” in partially observable environments, the very notion of a
497
      fixed FSM-like programmatic model may be fundamentally unsuitable, indicating that in
498   these cases, the representation itself is too rigid to capture the agent’s full intentionality.
499   Thus, we view ROTE as generating and reasoning over one of many possible representations
500   that are suitable for behavior prediction rather than a catch-all.

      A.1    Ground Truth Agent Behaviors for Construction
921
922   For research question 1 in Section 5, we hand designed 10 agents, represented as Finite State
923   Machines, to engage in diverse behaviors. The agents varied in complexity, with some using
924   sophisticated A-star search to achieve a goal, and others using faster, less resource-intensive
925   planning heuristics, namely the Manhattan distance as an approximation of how valuable an
926   action is for an agent looking to move to a target location. While there is a large body of
927
      literature and debate surrounding what it means to be goal-directed, in this work we say any
      agents conducting forward plans, denoted by explicit rollouts within the environment, are
928
      considered to be goal-directed. In the Construction gridworld task, this means the agents
929
      using A-star to complete tasks such as “pickup green blocks and move them to the corner”
930   and “pair all blue blocks together” are goal-directed, whereas “patrol the grid in a clockwise
931   direction,” which uses the Manhattan distance and FSM states as planning heuristics, are
932   considered scripted. In Partnr, all of the LLM agents are goal-directed since they use the
933   ReAct framework to plan how to complete household tasks. When collecting human data,
934   we do not know if the participants are conducting detailed planning. We note that while we
935   motivate our work from prior literature in cognitive science about predicting the behavior of
936   scripted agents, our empirical results demonstrate that our approach is robust to predicting
937   goal-directed behavior in the sense that we define it here. We summarize the behaviors and
938
      internal decision making states for all ground-truth agents below:
939
            1. Block Cycle: Using the manhattan distance as the planning heuristic, move from
940
               the green block to the blue block to the purple block to the green block and so on.
941            If the agent ever has a block in its inventory, it will immediately drop it and resume
942            its cycling behavior.
943
            2. Clockwise Patrol: If an agent is not along the outermost wall of the grid, it will
944
               repeatedly alternate between moving left and moving up until it hits a wall. Then,
945            it will follow the wall clockwise: if there is a wall above it, the agent will move right
946            repeatedly until it hits a wall, then repeats this process for going down, left, up and
947            right again. If the agent ever has a block in its inventory, it will immediately drop it
948            and resume its cycling behavior.
949         3. Counter-clockwise Patrol: This agent is the same as Clockwise Patrol, except it
950            it will patrol the border wall in a counter-clockwise manner, moving left repeatedly
951            until it hits a wall, then doing the same for moving down, right, up and left again.
952
            4. Left-Right Patrol: The agent will move left until it hits a wall, then will move
953
               right until it hits a wall, and repeat this process. If the agent ever has a block in its
954            inventory, it will immediately drop it and resume its patrolling behavior.
955
            5. Pair Blue Blocks: This agent uses A-star search for planning. If it does not have
956
               a blue block in its inventory, it finds and executes the shortest path to a blue block.
957
               Then, it uses the “interact” action to add the block to its inventory, and uses A-star
958            to find the shortest path to a different blue block.
959
            6. Patrol with A-Star: Here, the agent’s goal is to repeatedly cycle between the top
960
               left, top right, bottom right, and bottom left corners of the grid. While Clockwise
961
               Patrol has a behavior which on the surface may seem similar, for Patrol with A-star
962            we introduced addition complexity by having the agent believe it incurs a penalty for
963            touching any of the colored blocks. As such, it uses A-star with negative edge values
964            given to any action which leads an agent to landing on a colored block, thus resulting
965            in behavior which tries to patrol but frequently leaves and returns to the border to
966            avoid colored blocks. Again, if it ever picks up a colored block, it immediately drops
967            it.
968         7. L-shaped Patrol: This agent, initially at a coordinate (x, y), will move down until
969            it collides with a wall, then will move right until it collides with a wall. Then, it
970            will return to its original location, first moving left until its x-coordinate is x, and
971            up until its final coordinate is (x, y). It repeatedly does this process. The agent
               immediately drops any blocks in its inventory.

       A.8   ROTE in the Context of Existing Program Induction Methods
1280
1281   We also draw parallels to concurrent work that leverages large language models (LLMs)
1282   for program synthesis in cognitive modeling, such as CogFunSearch (Castro et al., 2025).
1283   CogFunSearch focuses on the mechanistic discovery of symbolic cognitive learning and
1284   decision-making algorithms (such as Q-learning with forgetting terms) in dynamic multi-
1285   armed bandit tasks, operating on large datasets across multiple species. Its methodology
1286   employs a high-cost, bilevel optimization, featuring a time-intensive outer evolutionary loop
1287
       to explore novel program structures (ϕ) and an inner differentiable loop to fit continuous
       parameters (θ). This high computational budget is justified by the complexity of simulta-
1288
       neously discovering program structure and fitting continuous parameters to capture subtle
1289
       learning dynamics. In contrast, ROTE is engineered for the real-time action prediction
1290   problem in non-Markovian embodied settings, prioritizing scenarios where data is sparse and
1291   rapid inference is essential. ROTE eschews the evolutionary loop and continuous parameter
1292   optimization, instead relying on an efficient, single-step generative process where the LLM
1293   synthesizes a constrained space of executable program hypotheses, often implicitly modeling a
1294   Finite State Machine, from sparse observations. This results in an executable representation
1295   that enables orders-of-magnitude faster long-horizon prediction by executing inferred code
       directly, bypassing repeated LLM calls. While CogFunSearch excels at high-fidelity mechanis-

1439
1440
       We will fully open-source our code, including the prompts we used for generating programs
1441   with ROTE across the various levels of structure. In Algorithm 1, we show the full algorithm
1442   for ROTE and subsequently discuss the implementation details. Our approach, ROTE,
1443   constructs the program space Λ using LLMs to synthesize executable Python programs,
1444   which serve as agent representations. These programs are structured as a class with a
1445   required act(self, observation) -> int method, ensuring a standard and executable format
1446   for all candidates. We intentionally avoid rigid, manually defined syntactic constraints
1447   across all domains to maintain representational flexibility, which is particularly important
1448   when modeling noisy human behaviors, and we analyze how these representations impact
1449

       theory of inductive inference and Occam’s razor, encouraging the LLM to generate concise
1450

1451

1452   to a maximum of 2000 tokens and restrict the number of generated hypotheses to N = 30,
1453   directly limiting the size of Λ and thus the potential complexity of any individual program.

1455   Machine (FSM) structure—which we permit, especially under the “Light” and “Moderate”
1456   structural conditions to accommodate inherent behavioral variability —the theoretical upper
1457   bound on the number of internal states, |S|, is equivalent to the size of the observation space,

1539       Handling Errors in Program Generation. Given that we are generating programs from
1540       smaller LLMs trying to adhere to a consistent Agent API, and that the observation space can
1541       be challenging to operate on, there are several cases where the LLMs generate semantically
1542       meaningful programs to describe observed behaviors that fail to compile or predict actions
1543       given an observation. As such, we explored two different methods for dealing with erroneous
1544       programs. The first was revision, where we prompted an LLM to fix the code it generated
1545       given the full error trace for a program’s prediction. We also gave it the original prompt and
1546       observations. The second method was completely resampling a program given the original
1547
           prompt, discarding the erroneous program completely. From preliminary tests, we found
           completely resampling was the more effective strategy given the LLMs we were using. Since
1548
           we paired this error correction process with methods like rejuvenation, we limited the number
1549
           of times we could resample or revise a program to be min(Max rejuvenation attempts, Max
1550       number of retries), shared across the rejuvenation and error corrections steps. This increased
1551       the likelihood of a good program which is executable being generated, without significantly
1552       slowing our single-step inference speed.
