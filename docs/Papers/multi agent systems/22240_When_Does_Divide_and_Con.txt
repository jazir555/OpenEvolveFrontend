011                                               A BSTRACT
012
013            We investigate the challenge of applying Large Language Models (LLMs) to long
014            texts. We propose a theoretical framework that distinguishes the failure modes
015
               of long context tasks into three categories: cross-chunk dependence (task noise),
               confusion that grows with context size (model noise), and the imperfect integration
016
               of partial results (aggregator noise). Under this view, we analyze when it is
017
               effective to use multi-agent chunking, i.e., dividing a length sequence into smaller
018            chunks and aggregating the processed results of each chunk. Our experiments
019            on tasks such as retrieval, question answering, and summarization confirm both
020            the theoretical analysis and the conditions that favor multi-agent chunking. By
021            exploring superlinear model noise growth with input length, we also explain why,
022            for large inputs, a weaker model configured with chunk-based processing can
023            surpass a more advanced model like GPT4o applied in a single shot. Overall, we
024            present a principled understanding framework and our results highlight a direct
025            pathway to handling long contexts in LLMs with carefully managed chunking and
026
               aggregator strategies.

028   1   I NTRODUCTION
029
030   Large Language Models (LLMs) have drawn significant interest from both industry and academia,
031   thanks to their ability to handle tasks ranging from open-ended question answering to complex
032   reasoning. As these models grow in capacity, there is an increasing demand to apply them to extended
033   texts that may span hundreds of thousands of tokens. In principle, self-attention architectures are
034   powerful, but their reliance on quadratic operations in sequence length can make long-context tasks
035   computationally expensive Tay et al. (2022). Moreover, even if a model technically can process long
036   contexts, studies have reported the quality decline of output once the input surpasses a certain length
037   (Hsieh et al., 2024). This limitation has been attributed to phenomena such as the “lost in the middle”
038
      effect, where the model forgets or mishandles portions of the input.
039   Existing research has explored ways to ease these difficulties. One type of approach has focused
040   on modifying the transformer architecture to reduce memory footprint and compute, often by, for
041   example, shaping the attention pattern through blockwise or window-based strategies (Qiu et al.,
042   2020; Beltagy et al., 2020), low-rank approximations (Wang et al., 2020) or routing-based approaches
043   (Kitaev et al., 2020), seeking to prolong the feasible context length without harming accuracy too
044
      severely. Although these technical improvements often extend the maximum input size, they do not
      guarantee stable performance when that size becomes very large.
045
046   A more functional approach divides a large input into chunks, processes each chunk with one or
047   more worker models, and then aggregates the outputs. Retrieval-augmented pipelines are a popular
048   example of this idea (Lewis et al., 2020; Fan et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b), but they often rely
049   on ad hoc rules for aggregating results. Their effectiveness hinges on how well global dependencies
050
      are preserved. If the aggregation step is weak, cross-chunk reasoning can be lost, which leads to
      suboptimal outcomes. While these chunk-based methods can lower confusion at the worker level, the
051
      completeness of the final result is not guaranteed.
052
053   In this paper, we propose a divide and conquer framework Qian et al. (2024b); Zhang et al. (2024c);
      Zhou et al. (2024) that offers a more systematic view at these issues. we analyze these challenges


                                                        1
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




054
      by establishing a theoretical framework that tracks three main sources of error in long-context tasks.
055   First, task noise arises from cross-chunk dependencies that cannot be handled by processing each
056   segment alone. Second, model noise arises from degraded performance with increasing input length.
057   Third, aggregator noise emerges when partial results are combined incorrectly, even if each chunk
058   was handled well. We show how the balance of these factors determines when chunk-based division
059   is advantageous, and how the design of the aggregator stage plays an important role. In particular,
060   when a task demands only mild cross-chunk reasoning, chunking can reduce model confusion with
061   little downside. However, when cross-chunk synergy is large, only a more advanced aggregator can
062   preserve important global connections.
063   We also show that model noise can worsen at a rate that outstrips the benefit of seeing the entire input
064   at once. Beyond a certain length threshold, splitting the text can yield better performance, even if the
065   individual worker models are weaker than a single large model. We attribute this to a superlinear
066   increase in model error growth with input length. Our experiments on retrieval, QA, summarization,
067   and other tasks confirm that this superlinear effect surfaces in practice, and demonstrate how a planner
068   can arrange prompts to keep aggregator noise manageable. Across these long context tasks, weak
069
      models handling chunks often outperform a single-shot strong model (e.g. GPT4o).
070   Our contributions are as follows:
071
072         • We present a theoretical framework for modeling error terms in long-context processing. This
073
              framework helps to explain when multi-agent chunking is advantageous and when it is not.
074         • We provide empirical evidence of superlinear performance degradation when input length is
075           large. We then demonstrate that splitting into smaller pieces can help mitigate these effects,
076
              except in the case where cross-chunk synergy is very high.
077         • We show that carefully designed prompts for both worker agents and the aggregator can stably
078           improve final performance. This allows smaller models to surpass more advanced models on
079           certain tasks when the input length is large.
080
      These findings broaden our understanding of how to tackle large inputs with LLMs. By breaking
081   down the main sources of error, our framework clarifies why chunking can help, how it can fail, and
082   how a planner can structure prompts so that partial outputs lead to correct final answers. Through both
083   theoretical arguments and experiments, we reveal practical guidelines for handling lengthy contexts
084   and show that well-planned division of labor can be a strong alternative to single-shot processing
085   with a massive context window.

087   2     R ELATED WORKS
088
      2.1   L ONG CONTEXT LLM
089
090   Developing long context LLMs has seen increasing popularity in applications such as long document
091   understanding. Due to the attention mechanism, transformer architecture is associated with quadratic
092   computational and memory complexity against sequence length(Tay et al., 2022), thus expensive to
093   train and serve in practice. To mitigate this, researchers have explored various efficient transformer
094   architectures, such as block wise attention(Qiu et al., 2020), window attention(Beltagy et al., 2020),
095   routing attention(Kitaev et al., 2020), etc. (Dao et al., 2022) proposed flash attention and (Liu et al.,
096   2023) designed ring attention, both contributed to efficient training of long context LLMs. The recent
097
      progress on transformer alternatives also show promises of overcoming the computational complexity
      of attention mechanisms, including Mamba(Gu & Dao, 2024) and Hyena(Poli et al., 2023). Also,
098
      given existing LLMs trained on short context, many research works have been presented on length
099
      extrapolation based on positional encoding(Chen et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2024),
100   and retrieval-augmented generation(Lewis et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024b), where additional design
101   complexity is be added on positional encoding adaptation and retriever. Besides, a complementary
102   line of work aligns LLMs for long contexts via preference/reward optimization, including LOGO
103   (Tang et al., 2024), LongReward (Zhang et al., 2024a), and LongPO (Chen et al., 2025).
104
105   2.2   M ULTI -AGENTS
106
107   Recent research has explored the potential of multi-agent systems utilizing large language models
      (LLMs) for complex task-solving. In the realm of task decomposition and planning, Qian et al.

      (2023) proposed a framework where multiple LLM-based agents collaboratively break down tasks
109   into subtasks and develop execution plans. Similarly, Haji et al. (2024) introduced a hierarchical
110   mechanism where tree of thoughts reasoners are combined with thought verifiers. Zhao et al. (2024b)
111   breaks down and process long texts using multiple agents, enabling efficient handling of 128K token
112   contexts through task decomposition.
113
      Beyond task breakdown and planning, recent work focused on communication mechanisms within
114
      multiple agents. Several studies (Guo et al., 2024) have investigated various approaches to enable
115   effective information exchange between agents. Common communication paradigms such as debate
116   is commonly used (Du et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2023). Alternatively, Qian et al.
117   (2024a) adapts a cooperative paradigm where agents work together towards a common goal. Many
118   multi-agent works use a layered structure. Wang et al. (2024a) developed a multi-layered framework
119   that separates LLMs into proposers and aggregators. Liang et al. (2023) encourages more divergent
120   thinking though layered debate among debtors. However, a centralized sharing structure has also
121   been explored by Hong et al. (2023).
122
123   2.3   D IVIDE AND CONQUER
124
125   Divide-and-conquer is a classic computational strategy that has recently been integrated with LLM-
126   based multi-agent systems to handle long-context processing. Recent works such as LC-Boost Qian
127
      et al. (2024b), Chain-of-Agents (CoA) Zhang et al. (2024c), LongAgent Zhao et al. (2024a), and
      LLM×MapReduce Zhou et al. (2024) , adopt this paradigm by splitting long inputs into smaller
128
      chunks processed by worker agents and then merging their outputs through a manager agent. These
129
      methods improve efficiency and enable long-text reasoning without requiring large context models.
130
131   However, existing approaches lack a formal theoretical framework to analyze the interaction between
132   task complexity, model noise, and aggregation errors, making it difficult to optimize chunking
133
      strategies. They also struggle with understanding how cross-chunk dependencies impact performance,
      often leading to loss of contextual coherence when aggregating local outputs. This work addresses
134
      these gaps by providing a rigorous modeling framework for analyzing noise propagation in Divide-
135
      and-conquer architectures, offering a systematic approach to optimizing multi-agent collaboration for
136   long-context tasks.

      3.4    T HREE REGIMES OF NOISES
217
218   In many practical pipelines, Φagg can be kept modest through careful prompt design by a planner
219   agent. Thus, the major interplay often arises between Φmodel (how confused the model is on each
220   chunk) and Φtask (how strongly the chunks depend on each other). We characterize three regimes.
221
222       • Case 1: Both Φmodel and Φtask are negligible. Here, each chunk is easy for the model, and the
223         task factorization mismatch is minor. Splitting into chunks or using a single shot yields similar
224         performance. Even if there is a slight aggregator noise Φagg , it stays negligible compared to the
225         small Φmodel and Φtask .
226       • Case 2: Φtask dominates. The input’s synergy is extensive, yet each individual chunk is
227         straightforward. A naive aggregator might fail to merge the global context effectively, causing
228         Φagg to blow up unless prompted carefully. In this regime, single-shot usage could still do well
229         if the model can handle long context, but D&C requires a robust aggregator for small Φagg .
230       • Case 3: Φmodel dominates. The task is nearly chunk-independent, but each chunk is intrinsically
231         confusing to the model when taken in a single large context. Splitting the input reduces the per-
232         chunk confusion, lowering Φmodel significantly. Even if Φagg grows somewhat, it is outweighed
233         by the model noise improvement. This is precisely the domain where Prop 3.2 shows that D&C
234         can outperform a single-shot approach.
235
236   These three cases illustrate that neither Φtask nor Φmodel alone determines whether multi-agent
237   chunking or single-shot usage is more suitable. Their relative magnitudes, together with ∆∗ (x) and
238   the aggregator’s effectiveness (Φagg ), ultimately decide which approach is advantageous.

470   6     C ONCLUSION
471
472   We presented a theoretical and empirical investigation of divide-and-conquer methods for large
473   context tasks. Our framework separates the final error into task noise (cross-chunk synergy), model
474   noise (per-chunk confusion), and aggregator noise (integration flaws). We showed that chunking can
475   outperform single-shot usage in many tasks where superlinear noise growth appears with context
476
      length. However, if cross-chunk synergy is too large or the aggregator prompt is ineffective, chunk-
      based approaches may fail.
477
478   Our experiments illustrate several key behaviors central to our noise decomposition framework,
479   including the sweet-spot trade-off between synergy (task noise) and chunk size (model noise), and the
480   conditions under which weaker models leveraging D&C can outperform stronger single-shot models
481   afflicted by superlinear model noise at large context lengths. We also showed that aggregator noise
482
      can be reduced by careful prompt engineering, which can be partially automated using a planner.
483   In future work, it would be interesting to explore more powerful aggregator models or retrieval-based
484   approaches that reconstruct the relevant cross-chunk information. We hope this framework clarifies
485   the interplay between context length, synergy, and multi-agent design, and provides a path toward
      improved performance on truly long documents.

1044   R ATIONALE FOR E FFICIENT E STIMATION
1045
       The feasibility of accurately estimating optimal chunk sizes from sparse samples can be understood
1046
       through our framework’s characterization of model noise. We posit that model noise (ϕmodel (x))
1047   exhibits superlinear growth with context length L = ||x||. It is reasonable to infer that this underlying
1048   noise function ϕ(L) is, in practice, often (or can be approximated as) monotonic with respect to
1049   L over relevant ranges. Consequently, within the D&C paradigm, reducing the per-worker chunk
1050   size is expected to lead to a predictably monotonic (or near-monotonic) decrease in the dominant
1051   accumulated model noise component, Φmodel .
1052
       When Φmodel dominates (and task noise Φtask is relatively small and aggregator noise Φagg is
1053   managed), the overall D&C system error as a function of chunk size is likely to exhibit a discernible
1054   optimal region (e.g., a convex-like shape or a region where decreasing model noise is balanced
1055   by slowly increasing task/aggregator noise) rather than fluctuating randomly and unpredictably.
1056   Therefore, a small number of samples across different chunk sizes can provide sufficient information
1057   to sketch the rough contour of this error curve, allowing for the identification of the approximate
1058   location of this optimal trade-off point without exhaustive evaluation.

1185
1186   We present a simple implementation of the framework as in Figure 1. Our implementation consists of
1187   three parts: a planner for automated query planning, numerous worker agents, and single manager
       agent. For a long context task, we divide it into chunks of approximately equal length and feed each

       chunk to one worker agent. Thus, we need to design how worker agents process their chunks and
1189   how manager agent could synthesize the appropriate answer from the interaction.

       M.1    W ORKER AGENT
1192
1193   In our framework, the long context sequence is divided into multiple segments, with each worker agent
1194   independently responsible for processing one specific segment. This design isolates the processing of
1195   individual segments, deliberately omitting any consideration of dependencies across segments. The
1196   goal is to simplify each worker’s task by restricting its scope to a single context segment. While our
1197   implementation employs homogeneous worker agents for simplicity, this setup can be extended to
1198
       accommodate heterogeneous agents with varying capacities in more complex scenarios.
1199
1200   M.2    M ANAGER AGENT
1201
       The manager agent is tasked with aggregating the outputs generated by the worker agents. It analyzes
1202
       the responses received and synthesizes a unified, refined output. For simplicity in our current design,
1203   we use the same model architecture for the manager agent as for the worker agents. However, this
1204   design can be adapted to allow for specialized managerial processing in more advanced applications.
1205
1206   M.3    P LANNER
1207
1208   The planner plays a central role in the coordination of the system. While we have outlined the basic
1209   structure of worker and manager agents, the specific responsibilities of each agent will vary depending
1210   on the task and the data. Instead of manually assigning tasks to the agents, we introduce a planner that
1211   autonomously determines the optimal distribution of jobs between the worker and manager agents.
1212
       The planner is given the original task description (e.g., "identify the second largest number in this
       list") and is responsible for generating appropriate prompts for each agent, taking into account the
1213
       segmentation of the long sequence. Moreover, human-designed agent workflows typically involve a
1214
       trial-and-error approach: initial job prompts are tested on a small dataset, the failures are analyzed,
1215   and the design is iteratively refined. We integrate this iterative process into the planner by having it
1216   perform an initial zero-shot job assignment, followed by an evaluation on holdout validation data.
1217   The planner receives feedback on mispredicted cases and revises the job prompts accordingly. While
1218   this iterative process can be repeated to improve performance, there is a risk of overfitting if carried
1219   out for too long. In our experiments, we iterate only once to avoid trivial problems.

1289
1290   For clear analysis and diagnosis, we adopt a control-variable design: we set the chunk size equal to
1291   the per-chunk context length and use equal-length, non-overlapping splits. This way, the performance
1292   change as chunk size varies can be primarily attributed to the length effect (model noise), rather than
1293   multiple moving parts at once.
1294
1295   Why not use adaptive segmentation in the main experiments? Adaptive policies (variable
       lengths, semantic boundaries, routing) simultaneously change:

              • the length distribution across chunks (affecting how model noise scales with length),

              • the boundary placement/routing relative to dependencies (affecting task noise),
1299          • the structure of worker outputs (affecting aggregator noise).

       When several factors vary together, the diagnostic curve (performance vs. chunk size) becomes hard
       to interpret, and we can no longer cleanly read off the relationship between per-chunk length and
1302
       model confusion, nor the relative contribution of the three noise sources.

       Benefits of equal-length splitting (control of variables) Equal-length, non-overlapping splits
1305

1308            read of Fig. 2/3),
1309          • stabilizes comparisons between D&C and single-shot across different ℓ,
1310
              • makes the diagnosis of “model-noise dominated” vs. “task-noise dominated” regimes more
1311
                reliable.

1313   Empirically, we also observed that small overlaps only yield marginal gains in our setting, suggesting
1314   the conclusions are not artifacts of brittle boundaries.

1316   The framework does not preclude adaptivity Our decomposition naturally accommodates adap-
1317   tive segmentation; when diagnostics indicate high task noise (strong cross-chunk dependencies),
1318   adaptive strategies can be useful in engineering practice. However, the focus of this paper is first
1319   to identify the length-driven effect under controlled conditions. In other words, we do not reject
1320   adaptivity; we fix chunk lengths to keep variables controlled so that the analysis remains interpretable
1321   and reproducible.
