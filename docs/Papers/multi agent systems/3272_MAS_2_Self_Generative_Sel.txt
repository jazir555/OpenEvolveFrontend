                                                 A BSTRACT
010
011            The past two years have witnessed the meteoric rise of Large Language Model
012            (LLM)-powered multi-agent systems (MAS), which harness collective intelli-
013            gence and exhibit a remarkable trajectory toward self-evolution. This paradigm
014            has rapidly progressed from manually engineered systems that require bespoke
015            configuration of prompts, tools, roles, and communication protocols toward
016            frameworks capable of automated orchestration. Yet, dominant automatic multi-
017            agent systems, whether generated by external modules or a single LLM agent,
018
               largely adhere to a rigid “generate-once-and-deploy” paradigm, rendering the
               resulting systems brittle and ill-prepared for the dynamism and uncertainty of
019
               real-world environments. To transcend this limitation, we introduce MAS2 , a
020
               paradigm predicated on the principle of recursive self-generation: a multi-agent
021            system that autonomously architects bespoke multi-agent systems for diverse
022            problems. Technically, we devise a “generator-implementer-rectifier” tri-agent
023            team capable of dynamically composing and adaptively rectifying a target agent
024            system in response to real-time task demands. Collaborative Tree Optimiza-
025            tion is proposed to train and specialize these meta-agents. Extensive evalua-
026            tion across seven benchmarks reveals that MAS2 achieves performance gains of
027            up to 19.6% over state-of-the-art MAS in complex scenarios such as deep re-
028            search and code generation. Moreover, MAS2 exhibits superior cross-backbone
029
               generalization, effectively leveraging previously unseen LLMs to yield improve-
               ments of up to 15.1%. Crucially, these gains are attained without incurring
030
               excessive token costs, as MAS2 consistently resides on the Pareto frontier of
031
               cost-performance trade-offs. The source codes are anonymously available at
032            https://anonymous.4open.science/r/MAS-2-3BB0.

      1   I NTRODUCTION
035
036   Inspired by the collective intelligence observed in human societies (Minsky, 1988; Li et al., 2023a),
037   large language model (LLM)-based multi-agent systems (MAS) have evolved into intricate ecosys-
038   tems composed of multiple LLMs, tool integrations, memory modules, and communication pro-
039
      tocols (Zhuge et al., 2024; Tran et al., 2025). In contrast to single-agent paradigms that seek a
      monolithic, all-powerful model capable of addressing every task in isolation, multi-agent systems
040
      harness the virtues of specialization and cooperative interaction among heterogeneous agents, with
041
      substantial advances across a spectrum of task domains, including scientific discovery (Ghafarol-
042   lahi & Buehler, 2024; Ghareeb et al., 2025), deep research (Zhang et al., 2025d; Hu et al., 2025a),
043   complex report generation (Yi et al., 2025), and collective reasoning (Ye et al., 2025a).
044
045
      The evolution of MAS has unfolded as a clear progression from manual design to full automation. (I)
      manually configured: Early frameworks such as AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023), MetaGPT (Hong et al.,
046
      2023), and AgentVerse (Chen et al., 2023) relied entirely on hand-crafted specifications, including
047
      prompts, agent roles, tool integrations, and communication topologies. (II) partially automated:
048   Subsequent efforts introduced partial automation: for example, GPTSwarm (Zhuge et al., 2024) and
049   G-Designer (Zhang et al., 2024b) automated the design of inter-agent communication topologies,
050   while MasRouter (Yue et al., 2025) and LLM-Selector (Chen et al., 2025a) focused on automating
051   LLM routing. (III) fully automatic: Recently, the community has converged on fully automated
052   MAS whose configurations are synthesized end-to-end and adapted online to domain and instance
053   alike, as exemplified by ADAS (Hu et al., 2025b), MaAS (Zhang et al., 2025b), and AFlow (Zhang
      et al., 2024c). This shift has inaugurated a phase of rapid, genuinely transformative advance.

068   Nevertheless, not all autonomously constructed MAS are alike. Early approaches often relied
069   on external modules such as Bayesian optimization (Li et al., 2023b), Monte Carlo tree search
070
      (MCTS) (Zhang et al., 2024c; Liang et al., 2025), or graph neural networks (Zhang et al., 2024b).
      While effective to some extent, these methods were confined to a predefined search space of atomic
071
      operators (e.g., Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Reflexion, Debate), limiting their capacity for architec-
072
      tural innovation. More recently, researchers have shifted toward enabling agents themselves to
073   construct MAS, employing techniques such as SFT (Ye et al., 2025b), DPO (Wang et al., 2025b),
074   and GRPO (Nie et al., 2025). These advances allow task-level adaptivity, whereby distinct sys-
075   tems can be customized for each problem instance. Yet most of these methods still adhere to a
076   “generate-once-and-deploy” paradigm, where a system is instantiated once per task and executed
077   unchanged (Zhang et al., 2025b), regardless of success or failure. This paradigm proves funda-
078   mentally inadequate, as real-world interactions between multi-agent systems and their environments
079   are inherently dynamic and error-prone (e.g., network failures, tool crashes, file loss) (Cemri et al.,
080   2025). Consequently, “generate-once-and-deploy” strategies are highly susceptible to collapse from
081
      a single unforeseen disruption and lack the capacity to adapt beyond their initial instantiation.
082   Having surveyed the evolution and inherent limitations of both external module-based and agent-
083   driven automatic agent systems, we propose a third new paradigm that inherently enables self-
084   generation and self-adaptation of MAS. Specifically, we envision a framework in which a multi-
085   agent system autonomously constructs another multi-agent system, which we refer to as MAS2 .
086   Unlike previous approaches, MAS2 orchestrates a specialized, LLM-facilitated meta-agent team
087
      tasked with generating, configuring, and rectifying systems in a task-adaptive and progress-aware
      manner. By internalizing distinct construction responsibilities across dedicated meta-agents, MAS2
088
      transcends the creative constraints of external modules and overcomes the rigidity of traditional
089
      “generate-once-and-deploy” strategies.
090
091   In this paper, we introduce a meta multi-agent system (meta MAS) designed to instantiate our pro-
092   posed MAS2 paradigm. This system is built upon a tri-agent architecture comprising a ♣ generator,
093
      an ❡ implementor, and a ♠ rectifier, each undergoing specialized training to internalize its dis-
      tinct meta-generative function. Specifically, for any given query, the generator agent architects a
094
      high-level, multi-agent workflow template, which outlines the sequence of agentic operations. Sub-
095
      sequently, the implementor agent instantiates this template by populating each procedural step with
096   a concrete LLM backbone, rendering the workflow fully executable. During runtime, the rectifier
097   agent actively monitors the execution state and environmental feedback, issuing timely corrections
098   to the system for adaptiveness to dynamic conditions.
099
      To facilitate training and data efficiency, we devise an offline reinforcement learning (RL) strategy
100
      for trajectory collection and optimization, termed Collaborative Tree Optimization (CTO). Within
101
      the CTO framework, the three agents collaboratively expand a decision tree representing diverse
102   MAS configurations and execution pathways. Upon reaching a terminal state, a final environmental
103   feedback signal is obtained. We then employ a path credit propagation mechanism to attribute the
104   final outcome to the upstream decisions made by each agent. This process yields role-specific pref-
105   erence data, which is then leveraged through relative reward-based preference alignment algorithms
106   to effectively specialize the distinct meta-generative function of each agent. In this way, MAS2 mate-
107   rializes a fully autonomous and self-adaptive multi-agent system pipeline, affording robustness and
      precision commensurate with the demands of intricate, long-horizon task.

      Automating Agent Systems concerns multi-agent systems whose configurations (including agent
123
      prompting, communication protocols, tool integration, and LLM backbone selection) are automat-
124   ically determined without substantial human intervention. Early efforts primarily relied on ex-
125   ternal control modules: for instance, GPTSwarm (Zhuge et al., 2024) and AgentPrune (Zhang
126   et al., 2024a) employed parameterized adjacency matrices; G-Designer (Zhang et al., 2024b) and
127   GraphRouter (Feng et al., 2024) utilized graph neural networks; while MasRouter (Yue et al., 2025)
128   adopted a variational autoencoder. A broader class of systems, such as ADAS (Hu et al., 2024a),
129   AgentSquare (Shang et al., 2024), AFlow (Zhang et al., 2024c), MaAS (Zhang et al., 2025b),
130   MASS (Zhou et al., 2025), and MermaidFlow (Zheng et al., 2025), similarly follow this paradigm.
131   However, the generative capacity of external modules proved inherently constrained, giving rise
132
      to model-driven approaches for constructing MAS. MAS-GPT (Ye et al., 2025b) employs large-
      scale query–workflow pairs for supervised fine-tuning of a system-generating agent, while Score-
133
      Flow (Wang et al., 2025b) adopts a similar strategy under DPO. FlowReasoner (Gao et al., 2025)
134
      and Weak-for-Strong (Nie et al., 2025), leverage GRPO-style online RL (Guo et al., 2025) to train
135   meta-agents for MAS generation. In contrast, our proposed MAS2 transcends the limitations of the
136   “generate-once-and-deploy” paradigm by enabling self-adjusting and resilient construction of MAS.
137
      Meta LLM Agents. Meta agents typically denote LLM-based coordinating entities that pro-
138
      vide meta-level guidance or generation for agent teams (Xiong et al., 2025). Most existing meta
139   agents are instantiated directly from off-the-shelf, powerful LLMs and are pervasive across do-
140   mains. For instance, in deep research systems, Camel’s OWL (Hu et al., 2025a), Skywork’s Agen-
141   tOrchestra (Zhang et al., 2025d), ByteDance’s AIME (Shi et al., 2025), Tencent’s Cognitive Kernel-
142   Pro (Fang et al., 2025), and several other frameworks (Bahdanau et al., 2024) all rely on a meta
143   agent to allocate and regulate tasks among sub-agents. Analogously, in software engineering sys-
144   tems, manager or “CEO” agents are widely adopted (Hong et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2023; Hu et al.,
145   2024b). More recently, a smaller body of work has explored explicitly training a meta agent as
146   a leader (Estornell et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). Distinct from these efforts, MAS2 elevates the
147
      concept further by transforming the meta agent from a single controlling entity into a meta-MAS.
148   RL for MAS. RL has been witnessed to propel the agentization of LLMs at an unprecedented
149   pace (Zhang et al., 2025a), substantially advancing their capabilities in reasoning, tool utilization,
150   memory management, and beyond. Within the specific context of multi-agent systems (MAS),
151
      existing research on RL for MAS can be broadly categorized into three streams: (i) training external
      modules, which leave the internal parameters of agents untouched while exclusively training external
152
      control modules via RL (Zhang et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a;d); (ii) training partial agents,
153
      which selectively train a subset of agents while keeping the remainder fixed, as exemplified by
154   MLPO (Estornell et al., 2025); and (iii) comprehensively training all agents that jointly update and
155   evolve all constituent agents, such as Sirius (Zhao et al., 2025), MALT (Motwani et al., 2024),
156   among others (Wan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025c; Park et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025).

      3.1            M ETA -S CHEDULING OF M ULTI -AGENT S YSTEMS
190   The effective deployment of an MAS for a novel, complex task necessitates a departure from static,
191   pre-defined architectures. Real-world problems are inherently dynamic, demanding a framework
192   capable of composing and adapting an MAS on-the-fly. Therefore, we introduce our MAS2 to dy-
193   namically construct a bespoke MAS tailored to the specific demands of a given task query, Q. We
194   begin by formalizing the constitution of a target MAS M as four core operational components:

198   T = {t1 , . . . , t|T | } is a set of available tools (e.g., python interpreter, playwright browser), and

      Rectifier. With the MAS M instantiated, it is deployed to begin execution. The rectifier agent Arec
217   assumes an online monitoring role to ensure system resilience. The agent is invoked if its trigger
218   function AR , which monitors the execution state st , evaluates to 1:
219

232   only custom-built for the task but also possesses the capacity for real-time self-correction, a key
233   advantage for tackling long-horizon, unpredictable problem domains.

264   3.3   T RAINING AND S PECIALIZING M ETA AGENTS
265   With the collaborative decision tree GQ fully annotated with value estimates from the above curation

268   node values. To leverage this, following previous relative reward-based preference methods (Gao
269   et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b), we leverage a value-guided optimization process to guide the
      training of the meta agents within MAS2 .

300   Benchmarks. To provide a comprehensive evaluation of MAS2 , we employ seven benchmarks
301   spanning four domains: ■ multi-hop search, including HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), Bam-
302   boogle (Press et al., 2022), and Natural Question (NQ) (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019); ■ deep research,
303   represented by BrowseComp+ (Chen et al., 2025b), an enhanced variant of OpenAI’s BrowseC-
304   omp (Wei et al., 2025) that corrects erroneous cases and enables more stable offline evaluation; ■
305   code generation, comprising HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021); and
306
      ■ mathematical reasoning, assessed with MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021).
307   Baselines. The baselines against which we compare MAS2 can be broadly grouped into three cate-
308   gories: ■ vanilla LLMs, where single models are directly evaluated, alongside prompting strategies
309   such as CoT (Wei et al., 2023) and Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023a); ■ handcrafted multi-
310   agent systems, including MedPrompt (Nori et al., 2023), MultiPersona (Wang et al., 2023b), LLM-
311
      Debate (Du et al., 2023), and DyLAN (Liu et al., 2024); ■ (partially) automated multi-agent
      systems, comprising ADAS (Hu et al., 2025b), MaAS (Zhang et al., 2025c), AFlow (Zhang et al.,
312
      2024c), and ScoreFlow (Wang et al., 2025b). For multi-agent system baselines, we instantiate them
313
      using the relatively highest-performing QwQ-32B and GPT-4o and report the average scores.
314
315   Parameter & Model Configurations. The LLM pool available for MAS2 is specified as GPT-4o
316   and GPT-4o-mini (Liu et al., 2024), Qwen2.5-72b-instruct (Qwen et al., 2025), Qwen3-14b (Yang
317
      et al., 2025b), and QwQ-32B (Team, 2025). We access the above models via the OpenRouter 1
      API. The backbone LLM for the generator, implementer, and rectifier in MAS2 are consistently set
318
      as Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025b). For data curation in Section 3.2, the generator expands into
319
      four candidate vG nodes, and each vG is further instantiated twice by the implementor, yielding two

455   The explanation of each devised multi-agent system is as follows:
456
      • (I) Bamboogle (QA): In QA tasks, the designed MAS utilizes multiple models (e.g., Qwen series)
457
        to generate candidate answers from different perspectives. Subsequently, a premier model (e.g.,
458
        Gemini-2.5-pro) takes on the critical role of evaluating these candidates to deliver a final verdict.
459
      • (II) HumanEval (Coding): For code generation, the MAS design embodies a division of labor.
460
        More economical, lightweight models are assigned to generate and test multiple code solutions in
461     parallel. Finally, a top-tier model (e.g., GPT-4o) is responsible for reviewing these initial proposals
462     and synthesizing a final, high-quality program.
463   • (III) BrowseComp+ (Research): In the MAS designed for complex research tasks, cognitive
464     roles are clearly demarcated. The most powerful model (e.g., GPT-4o) focuses on high-level
465     cognitive activities such as reflective reasoning, strategic planning, and information synthesis.
466     Meanwhile, more cost-effective models are employed to execute the mechanical, high-throughput
467     tasks of searching and retrieving information.
468
      Results showcase how MAS2 flexibly designs heterogeneous MAS tailored to domain-specific de-
469
      mands. In the QA scenario, various models generate diverse initial answers, while a flagship model
470
      like GPT-4o is reserved for the final, decisive judgment. In contrast, for code generation, economical
471   models handle parallel generation and testing, with the results ultimately being refined and elevated
472   by a more powerful model. In the research tasks, MAS2 dynamically coordinates models of dif-
473   ferent tiers to handle retrieval, summarization, and reflective reasoning. It is noteworthy that the
474   MAS presented here are generated solely by the generator and implementor agents. In Appendix D,
475   we further illustrate potential errors encountered by these MAS and demonstrate how the rectifier
476   dynamically corrects them, thereby validating the adaptive capabilities of MAS2 .

478   5   C ONCLUSION
479
      This work advances the frontier of automatic MAS by introducing MAS2 , a paradigm grounded
480   in recursive self-generation. Unlike prevailing approaches that follow a static “generate-once-and-
481   deploy” doctrine, MAS2 instantiates a meta-level tri-agent architecture, comprising generator, imple-
482   menter, and rectifier agents, that dynamically composes and adaptively rectifies task-specific MAS
483   in response to real-time demands. Through the proposed Collaborative Tree Optimization, these
484   meta-agents are trained and specialized, enabling robust adaptability and heightened efficiency. Em-
485   pirical studies across seven benchmarks demonstrate that MAS2 not only achieves consistent gains
      over state-of-the-art MAS but also scales synergistically with stronger LLM backbones.
